# Managers of Managers

## Audience

Use this guide for managers responsible for other managers, multiple teams, or a function that now requires consistency through leadership layers.

This includes:

- managers of frontline managers
- functional heads responsible for multiple teams
- senior managers running a department or practice area
- leaders who must deliver outcomes through other managers rather than direct individual-contributor control

Part of the art and discomfort of being a manager of managers is managing through people. To be accountable, yet to have delegated responsibility, takes steel.

That steel is not hardness for its own sake. It is the discipline to hold outcomes without grabbing every lever, to support managers without infantilizing them, and to intervene without quietly destroying their authority.

## Management Focus

The job shifts from personally creating clarity to ensuring clarity is created reliably through others.

Core responsibilities:

- build a consistent management system
- coach managers, not bypass them
- detect weak signals across teams
- align decision rights
- maintain accountability across layers
- prevent local optimization from damaging the broader system
- make management quality visible, teachable, and reviewable
- protect authority lines while still knowing what is really happening
- create escalation paths that surface risk without rewarding panic
- develop successor and deputy capacity across the management layer

The manager of managers is accountable for outcomes they do not personally execute and for teams they do not personally manage. That is the uncomfortable bit. It is also the job.

## What Changes at This Level

A frontline manager can often fix problems through direct attention. A manager of managers cannot scale by becoming the emergency substitute manager for every team.

The work changes from:

- solving every team issue directly → ensuring managers can solve team issues well
- knowing every detail → knowing which details matter and when to dig
- giving feedback to individual contributors → coaching managers on feedback quality
- running one team cadence → designing a management cadence across teams
- handling one team's priorities → balancing cross-team tradeoffs
- removing local blockers → improving the system that creates or hides blockers
- owning direct delivery → owning the reliability of delegated delivery

The temptation is to stay useful by staying too close to everything. That feels responsible. It is often just disguised control.

## What Good Looks Like

A healthy manager-of-managers layer usually shows these signs:

- frontline managers know what they own and what they can decide
- teams have enough consistency without becoming identical little process clones
- risks surface early across teams
- manager staff meetings focus on decisions, tradeoffs, and weak signals, not status theatre
- skip-level conversations reveal reality without undermining direct managers
- performance issues are handled by the right manager with appropriate support
- cross-team commitments are visible
- local optimization is challenged when it harms the broader function
- managers are coached on judgment, not just compliance
- succession and coverage risks are known
- the senior manager can explain where outcomes are healthy, fragile, or blocked

Good managers of managers create a system where strong managers get stronger, new managers get coached, and weak management does not hide behind polite reporting.

## The Steel of Delegated Accountability

Delegated responsibility does not reduce accountability. It changes how accountability works.

A manager of managers must be able to say:

- "This manager owns the team execution. I own the management system that makes that execution visible and supported."
- "I will not take the work back just because I am anxious."
- "I will intervene when risk, conduct, fairness, or delivery requires it."
- "If I override a manager, I will repair the authority line afterward."
- "I am accountable for whether my managers are capable, supported, and held to standard."

This requires judgment. Too little intervention becomes abdication. Too much intervention becomes bypass management. Both are expensive. One hides failure; the other manufactures dependency.

### Practical Test

When you feel the urge to step in, ask:

- Is this a manager capability issue?
- Is this a risk or urgency issue?
- Is this a decision-rights issue?
- Is this a system/cadence issue?
- Is this my discomfort with delegated control?
- If I intervene, what authority line am I strengthening or weakening?

If the answer is mostly "I would do it faster myself," step back. That is not a management reason. That is impatience wearing a blazer.

## Management System Design

Managers of managers create the management system through which work, risk, people issues, and decisions become visible.

A useful management system includes:

- clear team charters or responsibility boundaries
- manager role expectations
- regular manager staff meetings
- operating reviews
- priority and commitment reviews
- risk and escalation reviews
- skip-level listening rhythm
- people-development and succession reviews
- decision logs
- cross-team dependency tracking
- customer/stakeholder commitment tracking where relevant
- incident, retrospective, or learning-loop reviews

The goal is not process uniformity for its own sake. The goal is enough consistency that signals can be compared across teams and enough flexibility that teams can do real work without filing permission slips for oxygen.

## Manager Staff Meetings

A manager staff meeting is not a group therapy session for status updates.

Use it for:

- decisions needed
- cross-team tradeoffs
- shared risks
- blocked escalations
- people-management patterns that need support
- policy/process changes that affect multiple teams
- customer/stakeholder commitments
- operating metrics that need interpretation
- lessons from incidents, misses, or strong outcomes

Avoid:

- each manager reading a team update aloud
- solving every team's local problem in the room
- letting one urgent team consume every meeting
- hiding hard people issues behind vague language
- allowing managers to represent only their team's interests

### Example Agenda

1. Decisions needed this week
2. Cross-team priority conflicts
3. Material risks and escalations
4. Manager coaching topic or operating standard
5. People/succession concerns requiring support
6. Commitments and follow-up from last meeting

If nobody leaves with a decision, a clearer tradeoff, or a resolved escalation, the meeting may just be an expensive calendar ornament.

## Operating Reviews

Operating reviews help the manager of managers see whether the system is working.

Review:

- outcomes against commitments
- current priorities
- cross-team dependencies
- risks and mitigations
- customer/stakeholder commitments
- quality, delivery, support, or operational signals
- team health indicators
- hiring, capacity, and succession risks
- open decisions and accepted tradeoffs

The important question is not "Is every metric green?" The important question is "Do we understand reality well enough to make the next decision?"

Metrics can be useful. They can also become decorative camouflage. Ask managers to interpret the signal, not merely present the dashboard.

## Coaching Managers

Coach managers on management practice, not just delivery outcomes.

Useful coaching areas:

- setting expectations
- giving feedback
- handling conflict
- running 1:1s
- hiring and onboarding
- prioritization and tradeoff communication
- delegation and decision rights
- escalation quality
- performance documentation
- team cadence
- stakeholder management
- learning from incidents or misses

### Coaching Pattern

Use the same discipline expected from frontline managers:

1. Ask what the manager was trying to achieve.
2. Ask what they observed.
3. Identify the management decision point.
4. Connect behaviour to team or system impact.
5. Agree the next management practice to try.
6. Follow up with evidence.

Example:

> You escalated the delivery risk only after the deadline was already missed. I want to understand what signals were visible earlier and why they did not reach this forum. Next month, bring a risk review with early warning indicators, not just final delivery status.

That is manager coaching. "Be more proactive" is management-flavoured mist.

## Skip-Level Management

Skip-level conversations are for signal, trust, and calibration. They are not a secret alternate chain of command.

Use skip-levels to learn:

- whether priorities are clear
- whether managers are giving useful context
- whether risks are safe to raise
- whether feedback and recognition are happening
- whether workload is sustainable
- whether decision rights are understood
- whether cross-team friction is visible
- whether the manager's view matches the team's lived reality

Do not use skip-levels to:

- assign work around the direct manager
- reverse a manager's decision casually
- collect gossip as evidence
- promise changes before consulting the manager
- perform intimacy theatre with no follow-through

### Skip-Level Questions

Ask:

- What are your team's top priorities right now?
- What is harder than it should be?
- What decisions feel unclear?
- What does your manager do that helps the team most?
- Where do you need more context or support?
- Are risks raised early enough?
- What should I understand that may not show up in status reports?

After skip-levels, look for patterns. One complaint is a data point. Repeated themes are signal. Either way, do not weaponize quotes like a tiny internal tabloid.

## Bypassing and Authority Lines

Sometimes a manager of managers must intervene directly. Production incidents, legal/security risk, serious conduct concerns, customer harm, or urgent executive commitments may require it.

But casual bypassing is corrosive.

If you override or bypass a manager:

- explain why
- tell the manager quickly
- clarify whether it was exceptional or a decision-rights change
- repair the authority line with the team
- review what should change so bypassing is not needed next time

Example:

> I stepped in on the customer escalation because the response deadline was contractual and we had two hours left. That does not change your ownership of the support process. Tomorrow we will review why the escalation did not reach you earlier and adjust the trigger.

Without this repair, teams learn that authority is informal, proximity-based, and available to whoever can find the senior manager fastest. Civilization has made better choices.

## Decision Rights Across Teams

At this level, many failures are decision-rights failures between teams.

Clarify:

- which manager owns which outcome
- which decisions are local to a team
- which decisions require cross-team agreement
- which decisions escalate to the manager of managers
- what evidence is required
- what happens when managers disagree
- who communicates the final decision
- when the decision is reviewed

### Cross-Team Decision Example

Decision: whether to delay a release to fix a support-readiness gap.

- Product manager owns launch scope recommendation.
- Engineering manager owns technical readiness evidence.
- Support manager owns support-readiness evidence.
- Manager of managers owns the final tradeoff if the teams disagree.
- Decision record captures objections, risks, decision, owner, and review trigger.

The point is not to remove conflict. The point is to stop conflict from becoming an unlicensed reenactment of trench warfare.

## Conflict Between Managers

Managers should represent their teams without becoming tribal warlords.

When managers disagree:

- require each manager to state the other team's concern fairly
- separate facts, assumptions, preferences, and constraints
- identify the shared organizational goal
- make the tradeoff explicit
- name the decision-maker
- record material objections
- expect commitment after the decision

If a manager cannot argue beyond "my team needs this," coach them. If they still cannot, the issue is no longer only the decision; it is management maturity.

## Accountability Across Layers

Accountability must remain clear even when work moves through layers.

A manager of managers should inspect:

- whether each manager has clear outcomes
- whether managers have authority matching responsibility
- whether commitments are realistic and visible
- whether managers raise risks early
- whether managers follow through on people issues
- whether repeated misses indicate a local issue or system issue
- whether accountability is fair across teams

Avoid two common failures:

1. **Abdication** — "That is their team, so I should not get involved."
2. **Overreach** — "I am accountable, so I will make every meaningful decision myself."

The healthier position is:

> I will hold the manager accountable for their team, and I will hold myself accountable for whether they have the clarity, authority, support, and standards required to succeed.

## Weak Signals and Early Warning

Managers of managers must notice weak signals before they become obvious failures.

Watch for:

- managers reporting only good news
- repeated surprise escalations
- teams unable to name priorities
- rising attrition or transfer requests
- chronic dependency misses
- strong performers carrying invisible load
- meetings with no decisions
- managers avoiding feedback or performance conversations
- different teams interpreting the same goal differently
- customers or stakeholders bypassing normal channels
- quality problems framed as isolated accidents

A weak signal is not proof. It is a reason to look. Ignoring it because it is not yet a crisis is how leaders become very surprised by things everyone else saw coming.

## People Management Through Managers

The manager of managers should not take over every people issue, but they must ensure people management is happening well.

Review whether managers are:

- holding regular 1:1s
- setting expectations clearly
- giving timely feedback
- documenting material patterns
- handling conflict fairly
- recognizing good work specifically
- managing workload sustainably
- escalating HR/legal/conduct risk appropriately
- developing talent
- planning succession and coverage

Do not let poor management hide behind good delivery numbers. A team can ship and still be slowly turning into a retention crime scene.

## Performance Problems in the Management Layer

When a manager is struggling, diagnose the issue directly.

Ask:

- Is the manager unclear on expectations?
- Are they avoiding conflict?
- Are they over-controlling the team?
- Are they under-managing and calling it empowerment?
- Are they failing to escalate?
- Are they protecting themselves from bad news?
- Are they missing skill, judgment, courage, or context?
- Are they overloaded beyond reasonable capacity?

Support may include:

- coaching
- clearer expectations
- shadowing or role-play
- manager training
- reducing scope temporarily
- pairing with a stronger manager
- formal performance management where needed

Treat manager performance seriously. One weak manager can damage an entire team quietly and efficiently, like a very expensive leak.

## Hiring and Onboarding Managers

Hiring managers requires evaluating judgment, not just past individual-contributor success.

Look for:

- clarity under ambiguity
- fairness
- ability to handle conflict
- coaching capability
- decision discipline
- evidence of developing others
- ability to explain tradeoffs
- comfort with accountability
- willingness to surface bad news

### Interview Signals

Ask candidates:

- Tell me about a time you had to give difficult feedback.
- How do you decide when to escalate?
- How do you run 1:1s?
- How do you handle a strong performer with poor behaviour?
- How do you create accountability without fear?
- Describe a decision your team disagreed with. What happened after the decision?

Listen for specifics. Vague admiration for "servant leadership" is not evidence. It is often just a scented candle in sentence form.

## Succession and Coverage

Managers of managers are responsible for leadership resilience.

Track:

- critical manager roles
- deputies or backups
- successors and readiness levels
- single-person knowledge concentration
- teams with no credible temporary coverage
- managers nearing burnout
- managers whose departure would create immediate operational risk

Succession does not require promising someone a role. It requires knowing whether the organization can continue functioning when a key person is unavailable.

## Change Management Through Managers

Managers of managers translate change through the management layer.

Good change management requires:

- a consistent core message
- team-specific implications
- manager readiness before broad rollout
- space for managers to ask hard questions
- clear decision rights
- feedback loops from teams
- visible handling of risks and objections

Do not brief managers five minutes before their teams and call that alignment. That is just ambush with a calendar invite.

## Escalation and Risk

Watch for:

- managers hiding problems
- teams optimizing against each other
- repeated delivery surprises
- unclear decision ownership
- burnout masked as commitment
- customers or stakeholders bypassing managers
- repeated issues explained as one-offs
- managers escalating without options or recommendations
- managers failing to escalate material risk

### Escalation Standard

Expect managers to bring:

- situation
- impact
- options
- recommendation
- decision needed
- deadline
- tradeoff
- owner for follow-up

At this layer, escalation should become sharper, not louder.

## Results and Learning Loops

Inspect whether the management system is producing better decisions, healthier teams, and more reliable outcomes.

Ask:

- Are managers learning from misses?
- Are decisions improving?
- Are teams surfacing risk earlier?
- Are retrospectives producing changes?
- Are cross-team dependencies improving or repeating the same failure?
- Are people issues addressed earlier?
- Are customers/stakeholders experiencing more reliability?
- Are managers developing better managers beneath them?

The goal is not to prove the management system is elegant. The goal is to make it useful under pressure.

## Common Scenarios

### A Frontline Manager Keeps Getting Bypassed

Possible causes:

- stakeholders do not trust the manager
- decision rights are unclear
- the senior manager is too accessible as an alternate path
- the manager is slow to respond
- the team has learned escalation gets faster results

Response:

- clarify the manager's authority
- redirect routine issues back through the manager
- coach the manager on responsiveness or stakeholder trust
- repair the authority line publicly where appropriate
- keep a direct path only for serious risk, conduct, or urgent escalation

### A Manager Hides Bad News

Response:

- make the pattern explicit
- ask what they feared would happen if they raised it earlier
- clarify escalation expectations
- reward early risk surfacing, not cosmetic reporting
- inspect whether you are punishing bad news indirectly
- set a standard for future risk reviews

If managers believe bad news is punished, they will bring you fiction. Fiction is fun in bookstores. Less so in operating reviews.

### Two Managers Optimize Against Each Other

Response:

- identify the shared organizational outcome
- make local incentives visible
- force the tradeoff into the open
- decide or name the decision-maker
- adjust metrics or commitments if they reward conflict
- follow up after the decision

### A Manager Is Too Hands-Off

Signals:

- team members lack priorities
- performance issues linger
- commitments drift
- the manager says they are "empowering the team" but cannot explain current risks
- stakeholders bypass the manager

Response:

- distinguish empowerment from absence
- define required management cadence
- set expectations for follow-up and feedback
- review evidence weekly until the pattern changes

### A Manager Is Too Controlling

Signals:

- every decision routes through the manager
- team members wait for permission
- strong people leave or disengage
- the manager equates control with quality
- the team cannot operate when the manager is absent

Response:

- clarify delegation expectations
- require decision-rights mapping
- coach on risk-based review rather than universal approval
- inspect whether the manager is afraid of being held accountable for delegated work

## Artifacts Managers of Managers Should Use

Use artifacts that expose reality and improve decisions.

Useful artifacts:

- manager role expectations
- operating review
- manager staff meeting agenda
- skip-level listening notes
- decision record
- delegation agreement
- escalation note
- risk register
- succession risk register
- cross-team dependency register
- customer commitment register where relevant
- management-system review notes

Do not create a document for every emotional disturbance in the org chart. Create artifacts where memory, accountability, or decision quality improves.

## Anti-Patterns

Call these out early:

- bypassing managers casually and then complaining they lack authority
- treating delegated responsibility as reduced accountability
- solving every problem personally to feel useful
- confusing manager autonomy with absence of standards
- requiring consistency in trivial process while ignoring inconsistent judgment
- letting teams optimize locally against shared outcomes
- using skip-levels as gossip collection
- surprising managers with expectations they never saw
- tolerating weak management because delivery numbers look fine
- accepting green dashboards without inspecting weak signals
- escalating executive pressure without clarifying tradeoffs
- avoiding manager performance conversations because they are awkward
- mistaking silence from managers for health
- rewarding managers who hide risk until the last responsible moment has already fled the jurisdiction

## Review Questions

Use these questions monthly or quarterly:

- Do managers know what they own and what they can decide?
- Are teams operating with useful consistency?
- Are weak signals visible before they become incidents?
- Are managers surfacing bad news early?
- Are skip-levels revealing patterns or just anecdotes?
- Are authority lines being protected?
- Where are managers being bypassed, and why?
- Are cross-team decisions clear enough?
- Are manager performance issues being addressed?
- Are succession and coverage risks known?
- Are operating reviews improving decisions?
- Are managers developing their people, or merely extracting output?

## Bottom Line

Managers of managers are accountable for outcomes delivered through other managers.

That means building a management system strong enough to create clarity, surface risk, protect authority, coach judgment, and hold accountability across layers. It is uncomfortable because control is no longer direct. It takes steel because the leader must resist both cowardly abdication and anxious overreach.

Do it well and the organization gets stronger managers, earlier signals, cleaner tradeoffs, and more reliable execution. Do it badly and everyone gets a maze of bypasses, hidden risks, and heroic senior-leader interventions. Very dramatic. Usually avoidable.
