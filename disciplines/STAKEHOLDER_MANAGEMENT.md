# Stakeholder Management

<!-- doctrine:id=stakeholder-management -->

## Purpose

Stakeholder management is the discipline of identifying the people and groups who can affect, approve, block, use, fund, support, regulate, suffer from, or benefit from work — and managing those relationships deliberately.

Good stakeholder management helps an organization:

- understand who matters and why
- make decision rights and influence visible
- set expectations before assumptions harden
- communicate decisions, risks, changes, and tradeoffs clearly
- surface disagreement early
- handle conflict without turning every issue into politics
- maintain trust across teams, functions, customers, leaders, vendors, and partners
- escalate when alignment, authority, or risk exceeds the team’s control

Stakeholder management is not manipulation, flattery, or collecting everyone’s approval like ceremonial stamps. It is making the human system around work legible enough that decisions can happen before the meeting invites reproduce.

## Core Responsibilities

Stakeholder management is responsible for ensuring the organization can:

- identify relevant stakeholders and stakeholder groups
- understand their interests, authority, influence, needs, concerns, and constraints
- clarify decision rights, consultation needs, and communication expectations
- map support, resistance, neutrality, and hidden dependencies
- manage engagement cadence and feedback loops
- communicate clearly by audience and decision need
- handle expectation gaps, disagreement, and conflict
- escalate issues that require higher authority or cross-functional decision
- track commitments, decisions, objections, and follow-up actions
- preserve trust during uncertainty, delay, change, or failure

The goal is not to make everyone happy. The goal is to make expectations, tradeoffs, decisions, and concerns visible enough that work can proceed responsibly.

## Stakeholder Identification

Stakeholders may include:

- sponsors and accountable executives
- decision-makers and approvers
- people doing the work
- people whose work changes
- customers, users, clients, residents, patients, or beneficiaries
- managers and team leads
- support, operations, security, compliance, legal, HR, finance, procurement, and communications teams
- vendors and partners
- regulators, auditors, boards, or governance bodies
- skeptics, informal influencers, and known blockers
- adjacent teams that depend on or are affected by the outcome

Do not only map people with titles. Informal influence can matter more than the org chart, because humans apparently run on both hierarchy and gossip-powered routing.

## Stakeholder Mapping

Map stakeholders by impact, influence, interest, authority, and required engagement.

Useful dimensions:

- **Impact** — how much the work affects them
- **Influence** — how much they can affect the work
- **Authority** — what decisions they can approve, block, or escalate
- **Interest** — how much they care or need to know
- **Support** — whether they support, resist, or are undecided
- **Risk** — what goes wrong if they are ignored or mismanaged
- **Relationship health** — trust, history, and current sentiment

A simple map can classify stakeholders as:

- high influence / high impact: manage closely
- high influence / low impact: keep aligned
- low influence / high impact: engage and support
- low influence / low impact: monitor or inform

The map is a thinking tool, not a moral ranking of humans. Please do not become weird about quadrants.

## Stakeholder Needs and Concerns

For each important stakeholder or group, understand:

- what outcome they want
- what they fear
- what they are accountable for
- what constraints they face
- what information they trust
- what decision they need to make or influence
- what would make them support the work
- what would make them resist
- what commitments have already been made to them
- how they prefer to receive updates or bad news

Resistance should be examined before being dismissed. It may reveal real risk, unclear benefits, workload impact, loss of control, political context, previous damage, or plain self-interest. All are information. Some are even useful.

## Influence and Alignment

Influence is the ability to shape decisions, behaviour, support, or resistance.

Healthy influence uses:

- evidence
- shared goals
- trust
- timing
- clear options
- credible tradeoff framing
- listening before persuasion
- directness about constraints and consequences

Unhealthy influence uses:

- hidden side deals
- selective information
- pressure without accountability
- public surprise
- status games
- manufactured urgency
- relationship leverage to bypass decision rights

Stakeholder management should make influence visible and accountable. If decisions happen through back channels, the formal process becomes theatre and the real process becomes harder to improve.

## Engagement Strategy

Engagement should be proportional to impact, influence, risk, and decision need.

Define for each stakeholder group:

- purpose of engagement
- owner of the relationship
- cadence
- channel / forum
- information needed
- feedback needed
- decisions expected
- escalation path
- success signal

Engagement may include:

- 1:1 sponsor check-ins
- steering or governance forums
- working groups
- demos or reviews
- customer/user interviews
- office hours
- surveys or feedback forms
- readiness reviews
- executive updates
- informal relationship maintenance

Communication is not engagement if the stakeholder has no meaningful way to ask questions, influence details, raise risks, or understand decisions.

## Communication Cadence

A good cadence answers:

- Who needs updates?
- What do they need to know?
- What decisions or actions are needed from them?
- How often should they hear from the team?
- What changes the cadence?
- What happens during incidents, delays, or major decisions?

Cadence should vary by stakeholder type:

- sponsors may need decision-ready summaries
- delivery teams need detailed blockers and dependencies
- impacted users need what changes, when, and what support exists
- executives need risk, impact, options, recommendation, and ask
- customers need plain-language impact, commitments, and next update time
- governance groups need evidence and decisions, not vibes wearing a blazer

Too little communication creates anxiety and rumor. Too much creates learned helplessness and inbox compost.

## Expectation Management

Expectations must be explicit.

Clarify:

- what is committed
- what is provisional
- what is out of scope
- what can change
- what cannot change
- who owns decisions
- when decisions will be made
- what tradeoffs exist
- what risks remain
- how feedback will be used

Expectation gaps often come from polite ambiguity. Someone says “we’ll look at that,” and a stakeholder hears “approved for launch.” This is how small phrases become expensive little monsters.

Good expectation management means saying no, not yet, conditionally, or here is the tradeoff clearly enough that people do not have to infer reality from tone.

## Decision Communication

Stakeholders need to know not only what was decided, but why.

A useful decision communication includes:

- decision made
- decision owner
- options considered
- rationale
- tradeoffs accepted
- impacts
- who is affected
- what changes now
- what remains open
- review or revisit trigger
- where the decision is recorded

Decision communication should be adapted to audience. Executives may need strategic rationale and risk. Delivery teams may need operational implications. Customers may need impact, timing, and support. Everyone needs enough clarity that they do not accidentally keep acting on the old decision.

## Conflict and Disagreement

Stakeholder conflict is normal. The goal is to make it useful before it becomes political sediment.

Common sources:

- competing priorities
- unclear decision rights
- resource constraints
- customer promises
- risk tolerance differences
- professional/domain disagreement
- historical trust damage
- status or ownership concerns
- change fatigue
- incentives pulling functions in different directions

Good conflict management:

- names the decision or tradeoff clearly
- separates facts, interpretations, preferences, and emotions
- identifies who owns the decision
- records options and consequences
- invites disagreement before commitment
- interrupts triangulation and side-channel campaigning
- escalates when authority is insufficient

Avoid treating all conflict as bad. Some conflict is the sound of reality entering the room through a vent.

## Escalation

Escalate when:

- a stakeholder can block the work and alignment is failing
- a decision is outside the team’s authority
- commitments conflict
- risk exceeds agreed tolerance
- customers, regulators, legal, security, finance, or executives need action
- stakeholder behaviour undermines agreed work
- a team is being asked to absorb impossible tradeoffs silently
- repeated engagement does not resolve the issue

A good escalation includes:

- situation
- impact
- evidence
- stakeholders involved
- options
- recommendation
- decision needed
- deadline
- consequence of no decision

Do not escalate as revenge. Escalate because the work, risk, or relationship needs authority it does not currently have.

## Stakeholder Commitments

Track material commitments:

- what was promised
- who made the commitment
- to whom
- approval status
- due date
- evidence of completion
- risk or dependency
- change/expiry condition

Commitments made outside the official process still count if stakeholders believe them. The management task is to surface them before they become folklore with invoice numbers.

## Stakeholder Health

Review relationship and alignment health periodically.

Signals include:

- trust level
- responsiveness
- repeated objections
- decision latency
- surprise escalations
- missed commitments
- conflicting messages
- sponsor engagement
- feedback quality
- adoption/readiness
- sentiment in formal and informal channels

Stakeholder health is not popularity. A stakeholder may be annoyed and well managed if the tradeoffs are clear. A stakeholder may be cheerful and badly managed if they misunderstand the plan.

## Level-Specific Responsibilities

### Team Leads and Frontline Managers

- identify stakeholders close to the work
- communicate local impacts and blockers
- surface concerns and commitments early
- prevent teams from hiding stakeholder risk until launch

### Managers of Managers

- coordinate stakeholder expectations across teams
- resolve cross-team friction
- ensure managers communicate consistently
- identify when stakeholder conflict reflects unclear operating model or decision rights

### Directors and Senior Leaders

- manage senior stakeholders, sponsors, and cross-functional tradeoffs
- set engagement cadence for major initiatives
- make escalation paths real
- protect teams from conflicting executive demands

### Executives and Founders

- set strategic stakeholder priorities
- own high-stakes external, board, customer, investor, regulatory, or partner relationships
- communicate hard tradeoffs visibly
- avoid creating unofficial commitments that bypass the management system

## Disagree and Commit

Stakeholder management should create space for real disagreement before decisions harden.

People should be able to say:

- this stakeholder has real authority and is being ignored
- the communication plan is informing people too late
- a sponsor is not aligned
- customer expectations do not match delivery reality
- the proposed decision will create conflict in another function
- the team is managing around an informal blocker instead of naming it
- a stakeholder’s concern is valid even if inconvenient
- the escalation path is unclear or unsafe

After a decision is made, stakeholders should receive a clear explanation and the team should commit to one message. Disagree and commit does not mean pretending concerns never existed. It means recording the tradeoff and acting consistently unless new evidence requires a new decision.

## Common Artifacts

Common stakeholder management artifacts include:

- stakeholder map
- influence / impact matrix
- stakeholder engagement plan
- communication plan
- decision communication brief
- stakeholder commitment register
- escalation brief
- stakeholder health review
- sponsor alignment checklist
- meeting cadence map
- conflict / issue resolution note
- relationship handoff note
- audience-specific FAQ
- change impact assessment
- decision log

Artifacts should make relationships and decisions clearer. If the stakeholder map is just a list of names nobody uses, it has become LinkedIn with worse formatting.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

In stakeholder management, AI tooling will maintain stakeholder maps, summarize sentiment and commitments, draft updates, track decisions, and flag neglected relationships or conflicting expectations.

Practical uses include:

- summarizing stakeholder concerns from notes, tickets, surveys, and meetings
- drafting audience-specific updates from a single decision record
- detecting commitments made across emails, chats, and meeting notes
- identifying stakeholders missing from a communication or review plan
- creating decision-ready escalation summaries
- tracking open questions, objections, and follow-up actions
- comparing stakeholder sentiment over time

The risk is relationship cosplay: polished messages without real trust, context, or accountability. Managers still own influence, conflict, escalation, expectation-setting, and the human work of understanding what people actually need or fear.

AI-generated stakeholder summaries should cite sources, separate fact from inference, and avoid reducing people to sentiment labels. If a model says a stakeholder is “negative” because they keep identifying real risks, congratulations, the machine has discovered competent dissent and filed it under inconvenience.

## Anti-Patterns

Watch for these failure modes:

- mapping stakeholders once and never updating the map
- confusing communication with engagement
- treating resistance as irrational by default
- ignoring informal influencers until they block adoption
- allowing side-channel commitments to override official decisions
- over-engaging everyone and under-engaging the people actually affected
- hiding bad news to preserve stakeholder comfort
- surprising sponsors, customers, regulators, or operators late
- escalating as punishment rather than decision support
- giving every stakeholder veto power
- failing to name decision rights
- letting executive preferences become invisible commitments
- using polished updates to avoid hard conversations
- tracking sentiment without tracking commitments or decisions
- allowing AI-generated updates to sound aligned when reality is not

## Practical Starting Point

For a new effort, start with:

1. Identify stakeholders by impact, influence, authority, and relationship risk.
2. Name the relationship owner for each important stakeholder or group.
3. Clarify what each stakeholder needs to know, decide, do, or stop doing.
4. Define engagement cadence and communication format.
5. Record material commitments and open concerns.
6. Create an escalation path for unresolved disagreement or blocked decisions.
7. Communicate decisions with rationale, tradeoffs, impacts, and next steps.
8. Review stakeholder health before trust damage becomes visible from orbit.

The goal is not universal agreement. The goal is enough clarity, trust, and decision discipline that stakeholders can participate honestly without turning the work into unmanaged politics.
