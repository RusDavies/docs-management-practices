# Project Management

<!-- doctrine:id=project-management -->

## Purpose

Project management creates enough structure for a temporary effort to deliver a defined outcome without relying on heroics, vibes, or one exhausted person remembering everything.

A project is temporary. It has a beginning, an intended outcome, constraints, stakeholders, risks, decisions, and an end. The project manager's job is not to worship the plan. It is to make reality visible early enough that responsible people can make decisions before the deadline catches fire and starts giving interviews.

Good project management helps an organization:

- define the outcome and why it matters
- agree what is in and out of scope
- name owners, decision-makers, and stakeholders
- make dependencies visible
- manage risk and issues before they become folklore
- control changes to scope, date, cost, and quality
- escalate decisions at the right time
- deliver, close, and learn

## When to Use Project Management

Use project-management discipline when work has:

- a defined outcome or deliverable
- multiple contributors or stakeholders
- meaningful dependencies
- customer, operational, compliance, security, or financial impact
- a deadline or committed milestone
- cross-functional tradeoffs
- risk that needs active management

Do not over-formalize tiny work. A checklist, owner, and due date may be enough. The point is control, not decorative governance. Nobody needs a steering committee to rename a Slack channel, unless the organization has already lost the will to live.

## Core Responsibilities

Project management is accountable for making these things clear:

- outcome and value
- scope and non-scope
- assumptions and constraints
- roles and decision rights
- stakeholders and communication needs
- plan, milestones, and dependencies
- budget/resource assumptions where relevant
- risks, issues, and mitigations
- change-control path
- escalation path
- delivery evidence and acceptance criteria
- closure and learning

The project manager may not own every decision, but they should know who does.

## What Good Looks Like

Healthy project management usually shows these signs:

- the project brief is short, current, and useful
- everyone can explain the outcome in plain language
- scope boundaries are explicit
- milestones are tied to decisions or deliverables, not calendar theatre
- dependencies have owners and dates
- risks are discussed before they become issues
- issues have owners and next actions
- decision records capture important tradeoffs
- stakeholders receive the right level of communication
- escalation asks for decisions, not sympathy
- changes are assessed against scope, date, cost, quality, risk, and value
- closure captures what was delivered, what changed, and what should be learned

Good project management does not guarantee delivery. It makes the truth harder to ignore. That is most of the battle, and apparently still a stretch goal in many places.

## Project Lifecycle

### 1. Initiation

Initiation answers: should this project exist, and what are we actually trying to do?

Minimum outputs:

- project brief
- accountable sponsor or owner
- project manager / delivery lead
- initial stakeholder map
- initial scope and non-scope
- high-level risks and constraints
- decision required to proceed

Questions:

- What outcome is needed?
- Why now?
- Who benefits?
- Who is affected?
- What is the cost of not doing it?
- What is explicitly out of scope?
- Who can approve, stop, or materially change the project?

### 2. Planning

Planning answers: how will the work be delivered, governed, and controlled?

Minimum outputs:

- milestone plan
- dependency view
- risk/issue register
- communication cadence
- decision rights
- acceptance criteria
- change-control path
- escalation path

Planning should make uncertainty visible. It should not pretend that a colourful Gantt chart has negotiated with reality on your behalf.

### 3. Delivery Control

Delivery control answers: are we on track, what changed, and what decisions are needed?

Minimum practices:

- regular operating review
- status based on evidence, not optimism
- active risk/issue/dependency management
- decision log
- scope/change review
- stakeholder updates
- escalation where authority is insufficient

### 4. Transition / Launch

Transition answers: can the result be used, supported, operated, governed, and accepted?

Minimum practices:

- acceptance review
- production/readiness review where relevant
- support handoff
- training or enablement
- customer/stakeholder communication
- operational ownership confirmed
- residual risks accepted by the right owner

### 5. Closure

Closure answers: what was delivered, what remains, and what should be learned?

Minimum outputs:

- final status against intended outcome
- deliverables accepted or explicitly not delivered
- open risks/issues transferred to owners
- lessons learned
- benefits/measurement plan where relevant
- documentation archived or linked

A project that never closes is not a project. It is a lifestyle disease.

## Project Brief

The project brief is the starting control artifact.

It should capture:

- project name
- problem/opportunity
- desired outcome
- sponsor/accountable owner
- project manager / delivery lead
- stakeholders
- scope
- non-scope
- constraints
- assumptions
- success criteria
- major risks
- decision rights
- communication cadence

### Example Project Brief Summary

Project: Customer onboarding workflow refresh

Outcome: reduce manual onboarding handoffs and cut average onboarding time from 10 business days to 5 without increasing support defects.

In scope:

- onboarding intake form
- internal workflow routing
- customer notification emails
- support handoff
- reporting dashboard

Out of scope:

- contract changes
- pricing changes
- CRM replacement
- multilingual workflow redesign

Decision owner: VP Customer Operations

Key risks:

- Support readiness
- CRM workflow constraints
- customer-data privacy review
- Sales commitments already made to strategic accounts

## Scope Management

Scope defines the boundary of the project. Non-scope is just as important as scope.

Good scope management:

- defines deliverables and non-deliverables
- ties scope to outcomes
- records assumptions
- controls changes
- distinguishes must-have from nice-to-have
- makes date/scope/resource tradeoffs visible

### Scope Change Questions

When scope changes, ask:

- What outcome does this change support?
- Is it required or optional?
- Who is requesting it?
- What does it affect: date, cost, quality, risk, operations, support, compliance, security?
- What will be removed, delayed, or added to absorb it?
- Who can approve the tradeoff?

If the scope expands and nothing else changes, the project plan has not become more ambitious. It has become fiction.

## Milestones and Planning

Milestones should represent meaningful progress, not arbitrary calendar decoration.

Useful milestones include:

- brief approved
- design/architecture decision approved
- vendor selected
- requirements baseline agreed
- prototype reviewed
- security/privacy review completed
- pilot started
- acceptance criteria met
- production readiness approved
- launch completed
- closure review completed

A plan should include:

- milestone name
- owner
- expected date
- dependency
- evidence of completion
- risk/status
- decision needed, if any

## Dependency Management

Projects rarely fail because nobody made a schedule. They fail because the schedule assumed dependencies were domesticated animals.

For each dependency, record:

- dependency description
- owner
- provider team/vendor
- consumer team
- needed date
- current status
- risk level
- escalation trigger
- workaround or fallback

Dependency statements should be specific.

Weak:

> Waiting on Security.

Better:

> Security architecture review for customer-data export is needed by 12 July. Owner: security review lead. Risk: launch date slips if review is not complete by 19 July. Escalate to project sponsor if no review slot is confirmed by 5 July.

## Risk and Issue Management

A risk is something that might happen. An issue is something happening now.

Risk management:

- identify risks early
- assign owners
- assess likelihood and impact
- define mitigation/contingency
- set review dates
- escalate when risk exceeds project authority

Issue management:

- describe the current problem
- assign owner
- define next action
- set due date
- identify decision needed
- communicate impact

Do not hide risk to appear confident. That is not leadership. That is arson with a calendar invite.

## Decision Management

Projects need decision discipline because delivery work often crosses functions, priorities, and authority boundaries.

Use decision records when a decision affects:

- scope
- date
- budget/resources
- architecture/technical direction
- customer commitment
- vendor choice
- operating model
- security/privacy/compliance posture
- accepted risk
- production readiness

A decision record should capture:

- decision needed
- context
- options
- recommendation
- decision owner
- disagreement/objections
- final decision
- rationale
- follow-up actions
- review trigger

Decision latency is a project risk. If a project is waiting for a decision, that delay belongs in the risk/issue view, not in someone's optimistic facial expression.

## Stakeholder Management

Stakeholders need different levels of involvement.

Clarify who is:

- accountable sponsor
- decision-maker
- approver
- delivery owner
- consulted expert
- affected operator/user/customer
- informed stakeholder

Stakeholder communication should answer:

- What has changed?
- What decisions were made?
- What decisions are needed?
- What risks/issues matter to this audience?
- What action is required from them?
- When is the next update?

Do not invite every stakeholder to every meeting. That is not transparency. That is calendar vandalism.

## Communication Cadence

Useful cadence:

- weekly project operating review for active projects
- stakeholder update at agreed frequency
- risk/issue/dependency review
- decision review when needed
- sponsor check-in for material tradeoffs
- team working sessions for actual collaboration
- closure review after delivery

Status reporting should distinguish:

- on track
- at risk
- off track
- blocked
- decision needed

Avoid green status with red reality. Reality always escalates eventually, usually with worse manners.

## Change Control

Change control does not mean bureaucracy. It means not pretending changes are free.

For each material change, capture:

- requested change
- requester
- reason
- affected outcome
- impact on scope/date/cost/quality/risk
- options
- recommendation
- decision owner
- final decision

### Example Change Decision

Request: add multilingual email templates before launch.

Assessment:

- value: improves readiness for two strategic customers
- impact: adds translation, legal review, testing, support scripts
- options: add now and delay two weeks; add after launch; reduce other launch scope
- recommendation: launch English/French only now, add other languages in phase two
- decision owner: project sponsor

The point is not to block change. The point is to make tradeoffs explicit before the project quietly swallows them and dies of indigestion.

## Escalation

Escalate when:

- decision authority is unclear
- dependency failure threatens delivery
- risk exceeds project authority
- stakeholder conflict cannot be resolved at project level
- scope/date/resource tradeoff needs sponsor decision
- customer commitment may be missed
- project status is off track and recovery needs help

Good escalation includes:

- decision needed
- options
- recommendation
- impact
- deadline
- consequence of no decision

Bad escalation is just anxiety in bullet points.

## Level-Specific Notes

### Team Leads and Frontline Managers

Focus on:

- clarifying tasks, owners, and near-term priorities
- surfacing blockers early
- keeping work visible
- protecting team capacity
- escalating dependency and scope issues
- giving honest status

Typical artifacts:

- project brief
- task/milestone tracker
- risk/issue/dependency list
- decision notes
- team update

### Managers of Managers

Focus on:

- ensuring each project has accountable ownership
- resolving cross-team dependencies
- checking management systems, not just individual effort
- inspecting repeated delivery problems
- balancing capacity across teams
- escalating portfolio tradeoffs

Typical artifacts:

- operating review
- cross-team dependency view
- decision log
- risk register
- resource/capacity view

### Directors and Senior Leaders

Focus on:

- project alignment to strategy and portfolio priorities
- resource tradeoffs
- cross-functional conflict
- risk acceptance
- sponsor effectiveness
- customer/stakeholder trust
- organizational learning

Typical artifacts:

- portfolio/operating review
- sponsor brief
- risk register
- major decision records
- customer commitment register
- post-delivery review

### Executives and Founders

Focus on:

- deciding whether the project should exist
- setting strategic constraints
- resolving priority conflicts
- accepting enterprise-level risk
- protecting focus by not inventing surprise scope
- ensuring outcomes matter after launch

Typical artifacts:

- project brief
- executive decision record
- risk acceptance
- portfolio review
- benefit/outcome review

Executives should not parachute into a project, rearrange scope from memory, and leave. That is not sponsorship. That is drive-by governance.

## Common Scenarios

### Project Has No Clear Owner

Signals:

- many contributors, no accountable decision-maker
- meetings produce options but no decisions
- stakeholders disagree on what success means

Response:

- pause and define accountable owner
- create/update project brief
- clarify decision rights
- escalate if ownership cannot be assigned

### Project Is Green Until It Is Suddenly On Fire

Signals:

- risks are not recorded
- status updates are optimistic but vague
- blockers are discussed privately
- dependencies have no dates

Response:

- review evidence against milestones
- rebuild risk/issue/dependency view
- identify decisions needed
- reset status honestly
- escalate recovery options

### Scope Keeps Expanding

Signals:

- new requests enter through side conversations
- date remains unchanged despite added work
- team morale drops
- quality corners are cut

Response:

- log scope changes
- assess impact
- require sponsor decision
- trade off scope/date/resources/risk explicitly

### Delivery Completes but Adoption Fails

Signals:

- deliverable exists but users do not use it
- support is unprepared
- process ownership is unclear
- benefits are not measured

Response:

- review original outcome
- identify adoption barriers
- assign operational owner
- update training/support/change plan
- measure benefits after transition

## Disagree and Commit

Project decisions should invite disagreement before commitment, especially around scope, schedule, resourcing, quality, customer commitments, and risk acceptance.

Good project disagreement:

- is raised before the decision
- states evidence and consequences
- distinguishes preference from risk
- names options
- identifies who can decide
- records accepted risk

Once a decision is made:

- update the project record
- align owners and next actions
- stop re-litigating through side channels
- inspect results against the decision later

Use a [Disagree and Commit Record](https://github.com/RusDavies/docs-management-practices/blob/master/templates/disagree-and-commit-record.md) when the disagreement is material and needs memory.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

In project management, AI tooling will draft project briefs, update status narratives, flag dependency drift, reconcile plans against tickets, assemble risk and decision logs, and propose schedule or scope impacts. The practical gain is faster evidence assembly and earlier warning signs.

The risk is false precision: AI-generated plans can make weak assumptions look engineered. Project managers still own tradeoff framing, stakeholder alignment, risk escalation, change control, and acceptance of scope/date/cost/quality consequences.

## Anti-Patterns

Call these out early:

- treating the plan as reality
- hiding risk until the deadline catches fire
- mistaking status meetings for management
- adding scope while pretending the date is sacred
- escalating only after all good options are dead
- green status with no evidence
- project manager as note-taker only
- sponsor as honorary title with no decisions
- risks without owners
- dependencies described as hopes
- change requests handled through side channels
- closure skipped because everyone is tired
- launch treated as success even when adoption fails

## Review Questions

Use these during initiation, operating reviews, recovery, or closure:

- What outcome are we trying to produce?
- Who owns the result?
- What is explicitly out of scope?
- What assumptions are we relying on?
- Which dependencies can break the plan?
- Which decisions are stuck?
- Which risks exceed project authority?
- What changed since the last review?
- What tradeoff are we avoiding?
- What must be true for launch/transition to work?
- How will we know whether the project succeeded after delivery?

## Bottom Line

Project management is the discipline of making temporary work clear, owned, controlled, and learnable.

It should create just enough structure to help people deliver the outcome, make good tradeoffs, and avoid surprise disaster theatre. If the project-management process becomes heavier than the project, simplify it. If the project has no process and everyone is improvising under stress, add enough structure that reality has somewhere to land.
