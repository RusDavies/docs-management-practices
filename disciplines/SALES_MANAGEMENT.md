# Sales Management

## Purpose

Sales management defines how an organization creates, qualifies, progresses, wins, hands off, and learns from revenue opportunities without letting quota pressure quietly set product strategy, delivery commitments, or ethical standards.

It overlaps with customer management, product management, marketing, customer success, support, finance, legal, procurement, operations, and executive leadership. That overlap is exactly why the discipline needs explicit management guidance: otherwise every deal becomes a tiny emergency government.

Good sales management helps an organization:

- focus sales effort on customers the organization can serve well
- create a repeatable sales process without turning buyers into spreadsheet livestock
- maintain honest pipeline and forecast discipline
- qualify opportunities before they consume product, engineering, legal, and executive time
- make pricing, discounting, and exception decisions visible
- ensure promises made during sales are realistic, approved, and handed off
- protect trust through ethical selling and claim discipline
- align sales incentives with long-term customer and organizational health
- feed market learning back into product, marketing, and strategy

## Scope

Sales management may cover:

- sales strategy and target segments
- sales process and stage definitions
- lead/opportunity qualification
- pipeline hygiene and forecasting
- territory, account, and book-of-business ownership
- account planning
- buyer/stakeholder mapping
- pricing, discounting, and commercial exception governance
- sales enablement and messaging discipline
- proposal, proof-of-concept, and demo governance
- contract/commercial handoff
- sales-to-delivery/customer-success/support handoff
- sales compensation and incentive risks
- win/loss learning
- renewal and expansion interfaces, depending on the organization

Small organizations may combine founder-led sales, account management, product, and customer success. Combining roles is fine. Pretending the conflicts vanish because the same heroic mammal owns all of them is how promises become archaeology.

## Sales Strategy

Sales strategy should clarify:

- target customer segments
- buyer personas and economic buyers
- ideal customer profile
- disqualifying customer patterns
- sales motion: self-serve, inside sales, field sales, channel, partner, enterprise, or founder-led
- average deal size and expected sales cycle
- pricing and packaging assumptions
- markets, regions, or industries in scope
- required proof, references, compliance, and procurement support
- handoff model after sale

A sales strategy that only says "sell more" is not a strategy. It is a weather forecast with commission.

### Target Customer Discipline

Sales management should define who the organization should pursue and who it should politely avoid.

Good target discipline considers:

- customer problem fit
- ability to deliver value
- implementation/support burden
- regulatory/security/procurement demands
- margin and cost to serve
- product roadmap fit
- reference value
- churn risk
- ethical and reputational concerns

Bad-fit customers can produce revenue and still damage the organization. Revenue that burns delivery capacity, distorts the roadmap, and creates support debt is not automatically good revenue. It is sometimes a loan from your future sanity.

## Sales Process and Stages

Sales stages should describe observable buyer/customer progress, not salesperson optimism.

Weak stage definition:

- prospect likes us
- demo done
- probably closing

Better stage definition:

- problem confirmed with named buyer
- decision process identified
- budget or funding path understood
- technical/security/procurement requirements known
- compelling event or timeline identified
- decision criteria documented
- commercial proposal reviewed with decision-maker
- legal/procurement path active

A stage should answer: what evidence shows the opportunity has advanced?

## Qualification

Qualification protects the organization from spending scarce time on opportunities that are unlikely to close, unlikely to fit, or likely to hurt after closing.

Assess:

- customer problem and urgency
- buyer authority and decision process
- budget or funding route
- expected value and use case
- current alternatives/workarounds
- technical fit
- security, privacy, legal, procurement, or compliance requirements
- delivery/implementation needs
- support expectations
- timeline and constraints
- strategic value

Qualification should not be used to reject every uncertain opportunity. It should make uncertainty explicit so management can decide whether the opportunity deserves investment.

## Pipeline Discipline

Pipeline discipline keeps the organization honest about future revenue and required work.

Good pipeline management includes:

- clear opportunity ownership
- stage definitions based on evidence
- next step and date for every active opportunity
- close date realism
- deal value realism
- risk flags
- stale-opportunity review
- qualification/disqualification notes
- visible dependencies on product, legal, security, procurement, delivery, or executives

Pipeline reviews should ask:

- What changed since the last review?
- What evidence supports the stage and close date?
- What is the next buyer action?
- What risk could stop this deal?
- What internal dependency is needed?
- Should this opportunity still be active?

Do not manage pipeline by ritual optimism. The CRM is not a wishing well with columns.

## Forecasting

Forecasting is a management practice, not a vibes ceremony.

A useful forecast distinguishes:

- committed deals
- likely deals
- upside deals
- renewals/expansions
- at-risk deals
- slipped deals
- excluded deals

Forecasting should include assumptions and risks:

- buyer decision date
- procurement/legal status
- technical/security review status
- executive sponsor status
- competition
- budget approval
- required customer references
- internal delivery readiness

Forecast misses should be reviewed for learning, not only blame. Patterns matter:

- deals consistently forecast too early
- legal/security reviews underestimated
- procurement timelines ignored
- sales stages not evidence-based
- discounting used to rescue weak qualification
- product gaps hidden until late

## Account and Territory Ownership

Define who owns:

- named accounts
- territory or market segment
- inbound leads
- expansion opportunities
- renewals, if sales owns them
- channel/partner opportunities
- strategic accounts
- executive relationships

Ownership rules prevent duplicate outreach, neglected accounts, compensation fights, and the classic customer experience of being chased by three people from the same company who apparently met in a dark forest and never exchanged notes.

## Pricing, Discounting, and Exceptions

Sales management must make commercial exceptions governable.

Track and govern:

- discounts
- extended payment terms
- custom contract terms
- free pilots or proof-of-concept work
- implementation concessions
- support/service-level promises
- roadmap or feature commitments
- security/compliance commitments
- data residency or operational exceptions

Define approval thresholds. For example:

- standard discount: salesperson/manager
- material discount: sales leader plus finance
- margin-impacting or precedent-setting terms: executive/finance approval
- product roadmap commitment: product owner approval
- support/service-level commitment: support/operations approval
- legal/security/privacy commitment: relevant specialist approval

Discounting can be legitimate. Discounting to avoid saying no, hide weak value, or close bad-fit customers is just future margin erosion with confetti.

## Ethical Selling and Claim Discipline

Sales management must protect trust.

Good practice:

- make claims evidence-based
- separate current capability from roadmap intent
- avoid implying certifications, controls, integrations, performance, or security posture that do not exist
- document customer-facing commitments
- avoid pressuring teams to bless misleading statements
- correct misunderstandings early
- involve legal/security/privacy/product when claims touch their domains

Bad practice:

- selling roadmap as current product
- promising custom work without delivery approval
- using security or compliance language loosely
- hiding limitations until procurement or implementation
- treating buyer confusion as sales strategy

Trust lost during sales becomes churn, escalation, legal risk, or all three wearing a trench coat.

## Sales Commitments and Handoff

Every meaningful commitment made during sales should be visible after the sale.

Record:

- customer name/account
- commitment source
- commitment owner
- due date or trigger
- approval authority
- delivery/support/product impact
- risk or exception
- customer-facing wording
- handoff owner

Use the [customer commitment register](../templates/customer-commitment-register.md) for commitments that affect delivery, support, roadmap, commercial terms, security, compliance, or operations.

Handoff should include:

- customer goals and success criteria
- stakeholders and decision-makers
- promised scope
- promised dates
- contract and pricing exceptions
- onboarding/implementation needs
- support expectations
- security/privacy/compliance commitments
- open risks or concerns
- reasons the customer bought
- reasons the customer might churn

A sale is not complete when the contract is signed. It is complete when the organization can fulfill what was sold without relying on folklore.

## Sales, Product, and Delivery Conflict

Sales creates useful pressure. It also creates dangerous pressure.

Common conflicts:

- customer asks for roadmap change
- sales wants a discount product/finance dislike
- product says feature is not strategic
- delivery says timeline is not credible
- support says customer expectations are unrealistic
- legal/security/privacy block a deal term
- executive wants to win a prestigious but bad-fit customer

Healthy management:

- surfaces the tradeoff explicitly
- names decision rights
- records commitments and exceptions
- avoids punishing functions for telling the truth
- aligns on one external message after the decision

Disagree-and-commit applies after a real decision. It does not mean "sales promised it, so everyone else must pretend physics resigned."

## Sales Enablement

Sales enablement should make selling accurate and repeatable.

Useful enablement includes:

- ideal customer profile
- buyer personas
- problem statements
- discovery questions
- qualification guidance
- competitive positioning
- pricing/packaging guidance
- objection handling
- demo scripts and demo boundaries
- case studies and proof points
- security/compliance answer packs
- handoff checklist
- claim review guidance

Enablement should be maintained. Old decks are undead risk objects.

## Compensation and Incentive Risks

Sales compensation shapes behavior.

Watch for incentives that encourage:

- closing bad-fit customers
- excessive discounting
- hiding implementation risk
- overpromising roadmap
- ignoring churn risk
- selling unsupported use cases
- prioritizing new revenue while renewals burn
- pushing end-of-quarter deals that create long-term damage

Compensation design is not only a finance problem. It is a management-control system.

## Metrics

Useful sales-management metrics may include:

- qualified pipeline by segment
- conversion by stage
- cycle time by deal type
- forecast accuracy
- win/loss reasons
- average discount and discount reason
- deal margin or expected cost to serve
- stale opportunity count
- handoff completeness
- customer commitments created during sales
- early churn or implementation failure by source/segment
- sales-sourced product requests and outcomes

Do not optimize one metric in isolation. A team can improve close rate by pursuing only tiny deals, improve bookings by discounting recklessly, or improve forecast by sandbagging. Metrics are useful servants and terrible little emperors.

## Level-Specific Notes

### Team leads and frontline managers

- coach discovery, qualification, and next-step discipline
- inspect pipeline evidence, not only stage labels
- review commitments before they leave the sales team
- reinforce ethical claims and accurate demos
- help reps learn from losses without making every loss a tribunal

### Managers of managers

- standardize stage definitions and forecast practice
- review territory/account ownership patterns
- identify systemic handoff failures
- coordinate with product, marketing, customer success, support, finance, and legal
- tune process without burying sellers in admin theatre

### Directors and senior leaders

- align sales strategy with product and market strategy
- govern discounting, exceptions, and strategic deals
- manage capacity tradeoffs across sales, delivery, and support
- review incentive risks
- ensure sales learning informs strategy

### Executives and founders

- decide which customers and deals the organization should not pursue
- model claim discipline and ethical selling
- approve major exceptions consciously
- avoid using executive pressure to bypass product, legal, security, or delivery reality
- connect revenue ambition to organizational capability

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

In sales management, AI tooling will summarize account history, draft outreach, prepare call plans, score opportunities, flag stale pipeline, generate proposal material, and surface win/loss patterns. The useful version improves preparation, qualification, and handoff quality.

The risk is automated optimism: generated pipeline narratives, over-personalized outreach, or proposal language that quietly exceeds approved claims. Sales leaders still own ethical selling, forecast judgment, discount/exception approval, customer commitments, and handoff accountability.

## Anti-Patterns

- treating pipeline as optimism storage
- confusing activity with progress
- rewarding bookings while ignoring customer fit
- discounting instead of diagnosing weak value
- selling roadmap as current capability
- making delivery, support, legal, or security discover commitments after signature
- letting one loud strategic prospect hijack the roadmap
- punishing accurate forecasts because they are inconvenient
- measuring sales only by closed revenue and ignoring churn, margin, and support burden
- burying salespeople in process that does not improve selling or governance
- allowing heroic founder sales to create commitments no operating model can support

## Review Questions

- Are sales stages based on observable customer progress?
- Do we know which customers we should not pursue?
- Are discounts and exceptions visible and approved at the right level?
- Are customer commitments handed off before implementation or support begins?
- Do sales claims match current product capability and approved roadmap language?
- Are forecast misses teaching us anything?
- Are incentives creating behavior we actually want?
- Do product, delivery, support, and customer success trust the sales process?
- Are we winning deals we can serve well?
