# Funding Management

<!-- doctrine:id=funding-management -->

## Purpose

Funding management is the discipline of managing how an organization acquires, prepares for, governs, negotiates, accepts, and remains accountable for external or internal funding and investment.

It covers the management system around grants, investment, loans, sponsorship, philanthropic funding, strategic funding, public-sector funding, venture financing, project funding, program funding, and other capital or resource acquisition paths. It is concerned with strategy, readiness, evidence, pipeline governance, decision rights, claims, commitments, diligence, relationship ownership, compliance, and post-funding obligations.

Funding management is not the same as finance operations, accounting, fundraising execution, grant writing, investor relations work, or sales. Those may be practitioner activities or adjacent functions. Funding management defines the leadership, governance, approval, evidence, and accountability frame around acquiring and accepting funding.

Good funding management helps an organization:

- connect funding activity to strategy and mission
- choose the right funding paths for the organization’s stage, constraints, risk, and values
- prepare credible evidence before making funding claims
- manage funder, investor, lender, sponsor, or grantor pipelines deliberately
- define who can make commitments, representations, concessions, or use-of-funds promises
- coordinate diligence, legal, finance, product, research, operations, risk, compliance, and communications
- avoid taking attractive money that quietly imports terrible obligations
- preserve post-funding accountability so acquisition does not become amnesia with a wire transfer

Funding is not free because the cheque cleared. Money arrives with expectations, constraints, reporting obligations, dilution, repayment risk, reputational exposure, governance demands, or at least someone who will want a deck at the least convenient moment.

## Scope

Funding management may cover:

- funding strategy and target funding mix
- funding readiness and evidence standards
- investor, funder, lender, sponsor, donor, or grantor targeting
- funding pipeline governance
- use-of-funds planning
- business case and impact case review
- pitch, proposal, grant, and diligence readiness
- valuation, terms, covenants, restrictions, and control implications
- commitment and claims approval
- relationship ownership and communication cadence
- legal, finance, tax, compliance, risk, privacy, security, research, and operational interfaces
- conflicts of interest and independence boundaries
- funding decision rights and acceptance gates
- post-funding reporting, obligations, milestones, and stewardship
- renewal, follow-on, bridge, or exit planning
- funding knowledge capture and reuse

The scope should match the funding path. A small internal innovation budget does not need venture financing ceremony. A regulated grant, institutional investment, loan covenant, public claim, or strategic funder relationship should not be run from someone’s inbox and optimism.

## Funding Management vs Resource and Financial Management

Resource and Financial Management covers how the organization allocates, budgets, forecasts, and manages money and capacity once resources exist or are planned.

Funding Management covers how the organization obtains or renews funding and investment, and what obligations it accepts in doing so.

They overlap at:

- budget and forecast evidence
- use-of-funds plans
- affordability and runway
- cash timing
- financial controls
- variance and milestone reporting
- funding constraints on portfolio choices

Keep the boundary explicit. Funding management should not invent financial projections without finance ownership. Finance should not accept funding terms or external promises without the accountable business, legal, risk, and strategy owners. Otherwise everyone gets to be surprised later, which is apparently a popular but poor governance model.

## Funding Strategy

Funding strategy should explain what funding is needed, why, from whom, under what constraints, and with what tradeoffs.

Define:

- strategic purpose of funding
- target amount or resource envelope
- timing and runway need
- acceptable funding sources
- unacceptable or high-risk sources
- desired funding mix
- cost of capital or obligation profile
- control, governance, dilution, repayment, restriction, or reporting tradeoffs
- readiness gaps
- relationship strategy
- approval path

Different funding sources behave differently:

- equity investment may bring dilution, governance rights, investor expectations, and future fundraising pressure
- debt may bring repayment obligations, covenants, and cash-flow risk
- grants may bring eligibility, reporting, audit, spending restrictions, and public-interest obligations
- sponsorship may bring brand, independence, and claim constraints
- philanthropic funding may bring mission alignment, stewardship, and donor restrictions
- internal funding may bring portfolio tradeoffs and executive accountability
- strategic partnerships may bring dependency, exclusivity, or roadmap influence

The best funding path is not always the largest cheque. Sometimes the expensive money is the cheap-looking money with hooks.

## Funding Readiness

Funding readiness means the organization can credibly support the ask and survive the obligations.

Readiness evidence may include:

- strategy and use-of-funds rationale
- problem/opportunity evidence
- market, customer, user, community, or beneficiary evidence
- research or technical evidence
- financial model, budget, and forecast
- operating plan and milestones
- team and governance credibility
- legal entity and cap table readiness where relevant
- compliance, privacy, security, or regulatory posture
- prior results, traction, impact, or delivery evidence
- risks, assumptions, and mitigations
- diligence packet or data room
- reporting and stewardship capability

Do not confuse an exciting story with readiness. A story gets the conversation. Evidence survives diligence.

## Funding Pipeline Governance

Manage funding opportunities as a pipeline.

Capture:

- funding source or prospect
- type: grant, investor, lender, sponsor, donor, internal allocation, strategic partner
- owner
- stage
- amount or range
- timing
- eligibility or fit
- strategic value
- restrictions or obligations
- probability/confidence
- required materials
- next action and date
- decision owner
- risk notes

Pipeline stages might include:

1. identified
2. qualified
3. relationship opened
4. materials requested/prepared
5. submitted/pitched
6. diligence/review
7. terms/award negotiation
8. approved/accepted
9. declined/lost/deferred
10. active stewardship/reporting

A funding pipeline should support decision-making, not merely cheerleading. If every prospect is high priority and every stage is “promising,” the pipeline is a horoscope with currency symbols.

## Targeting and Qualification

Funding sources should be qualified before serious pursuit.

Evaluate:

- strategic fit
- amount and timing fit
- eligibility
- decision process and timeline
- required evidence
- restrictions and obligations
- reputation and alignment
- cost of pursuit
- relationship leverage
- probability
- downstream reporting burden
- conflicts or independence concerns

Bad-fit funding consumes attention, creates distorted positioning, and may pull the organization toward work it should not do. Chasing every available grant, investor, or sponsor is not hustle. It is strategic attention wandering around with a net.

## Use-of-Funds Planning

Funding asks should explain what the money will do.

A use-of-funds plan should clarify:

- funded activities or workstreams
- owners
- timeline
- milestones or deliverables
- expected outcomes
- operating vs capital spend
- people, vendor, tool, research, product, compliance, or operational needs
- contingency or reserves
- restricted vs unrestricted use
- dependencies and risks
- reporting measures

The plan should be realistic enough that delivery teams, finance, operations, and leaders can recognize it. A funding use plan that looks wonderful only because the people doing the work have not seen it yet is not ready.

## Claims, Representations, and Commitments

Funding activity often requires claims about the organization, product, research, market, impact, finances, compliance, security, team, or future plans.

Define who can approve:

- financial claims and projections
- market, traction, customer, or impact claims
- research or technical claims
- legal, regulatory, privacy, security, or compliance claims
- product roadmap or delivery commitments
- hiring, capacity, or operating commitments
- use-of-funds promises
- reporting obligations
- exclusivity, control, governance, or rights concessions
- public statements and announcements

Claims should be tied to evidence and review. A pitch deck is not a magical zone where unsupported certainty becomes charming instead of risky.

## Diligence and Evidence Packets

Funding diligence should be managed as a controlled evidence process.

Common evidence areas:

- corporate/entity information
- ownership/cap table where relevant
- financial statements, budgets, forecasts, and runway
- customer, market, user, beneficiary, or impact evidence
- product, technology, architecture, and roadmap
- research evidence and publications where relevant
- legal contracts and obligations
- intellectual property
- privacy, security, compliance, and risk posture
- team, hiring plan, and governance
- vendor and partner dependencies
- prior funding and reporting history
- policies, controls, and audit evidence

Assign owners for each evidence area. Keep materials current, access-controlled, and versioned. Track what was shared, with whom, under what confidentiality terms, and when.

Diligence is where unsupported stories become work. Better to find the gap before the funder, investor, lender, or grant reviewer finds it with a polite email and a spreadsheet attachment.

## Terms, Restrictions, and Acceptance Gates

Funding should not be accepted only because it is available.

Review:

- amount and timing
- cost of capital
- repayment obligations
- equity dilution or governance rights
- covenants
- spending restrictions
- reporting requirements
- audit rights
- milestone obligations
- publication or communication obligations
- intellectual-property terms
- exclusivity, most-favoured-nation, or control provisions
- termination, clawback, or default consequences
- conflicts with mission, strategy, existing obligations, or customers

Acceptance gates should involve the right owners: executive/business owner, finance, legal, risk, compliance, security/privacy where relevant, operational owner, and any domain owner whose commitments are affected.

The correct answer to some funding offers is no. This is deeply offensive to people who enjoy large numbers, but still true.

## Relationship Ownership

Funding relationships need owners.

Clarify:

- primary relationship owner
- executive sponsor where needed
- finance owner
- legal/contract owner
- reporting owner
- operating/delivery owner
- communications owner
- escalation path
- cadence and touchpoints
- renewal/follow-on ownership

Funding relationships are not only transactional. They can shape reputation, future opportunity, governance, public trust, customer confidence, and strategic optionality. They can also become weirdly needy if nobody owns the relationship after the announcement photo.

## Post-Funding Accountability

After funding is accepted, the organization must manage obligations.

Track:

- milestone commitments
- reporting deadlines
- spending restrictions
- financial variance
- outcomes or impact measures
- governance or board/reporting obligations
- audit evidence
- renewal or follow-on conditions
- communication commitments
- risk or issue escalation
- closeout requirements

Funding management should hand off operational tracking to the right owners while preserving management accountability. Getting funded is not the finish line. It is the point where promises start asking for receipts.

## Funding Practitioner Boundary

For detailed practitioner scope, see [Funding Practitioner](https://github.com/RusDavies/docs-practitioner-practices/blob/master/roles/funding-practitioner.md).


Funding practitioners perform hands-on funding acquisition work:

- prospect research
- grant/proposal drafting
- investor or lender materials
- sponsor or donor materials
- data-room upkeep
- diligence packet preparation
- outreach coordination
- follow-up tracking
- financial/evidence collection support
- CRM or pipeline updates

Funding management owns the management frame:

- funding strategy
- fit and qualification decisions
- approval boundaries
- claims and commitment review
- terms and acceptance gates
- relationship ownership
- post-funding accountability
- escalation and governance

A person may perform both roles in a small organization. The active scope must still be explicit. The person drafting a proposal should not silently approve the financial projection, legal representation, security claim, delivery commitment, and acceptance of restrictions because the deadline is today and the portal is unpleasant.

## Interfaces With Other Disciplines

Funding management often interfaces with:

- Resource and Financial Management for budgets, forecasts, runway, cost ownership, and variance
- Portfolio Management for investment choices and strategic allocation
- Program and Project Management for funded delivery plans and milestones
- Research Management for research evidence, sponsor constraints, and publication boundaries
- Product Management for market, roadmap, launch, and customer evidence
- Sales and Marketing/GTM Management for positioning, claims, demand, and communication
- Vendor and Partner Management for strategic funder/partner relationships
- Procurement Management for externally funded purchases or supplier obligations
- Risk, Compliance, Legal, Security, and Privacy for diligence, controls, obligations, and exposure
- Knowledge Management for reusable evidence, decision records, and funding lessons learned

Funding activity should not bypass these disciplines because money is urgent. Urgency is precisely when organizations make expensive promises with theatrical confidence.

## Metrics and Review Cadence

Useful funding management measures include:

- funding pipeline value by stage and type
- qualified vs unqualified opportunities
- pursuit cost and time
- win/loss/decline reasons
- readiness gaps
- diligence response time
- evidence packet freshness
- commitment and reporting obligation health
- post-funding milestone status
- restriction or covenant compliance
- renewal/follow-on probability
- concentration risk by funding source
- forecast vs actual funded outcomes

Measure quality, not just activity. A team can submit many proposals, pitch many investors, and still build a funding machine that mostly produces exhaustion and inbox sediment.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

AI tools can help with funder/prospect research, opportunity matching, draft proposal language, diligence checklist generation, data-room indexing, prior-answer retrieval, claim consistency checks, follow-up tracking, and summarizing funder requirements.

AI tools also create risks:

- hallucinated funder criteria or contact details
- unsupported claims in proposals or decks
- accidental disclosure of confidential financial, customer, employee, research, or security information
- over-polished language that hides weak evidence
- inconsistent answers across diligence materials
- loss of provenance for claims
- reuse of restricted or funder-specific materials in the wrong context

Funding management should define allowed AI uses, data boundaries, human review requirements, source/provenance expectations, and approval gates for any externally shared material.

AI can draft a proposal paragraph. It cannot authorize the claim, accept the covenant, or make cash-flow physics less annoying.

## Anti-Patterns

Watch for these failure modes:

- chasing funding that does not fit strategy or mission
- treating the largest cheque as the best option
- making unsupported market, impact, research, compliance, security, or financial claims
- letting a proposal deadline override legal, finance, risk, or domain review
- accepting restricted funding without understanding delivery and reporting burden
- burying obligations after funds arrive
- letting funder or investor pressure distort research, product, or operational priorities
- running funding work from private inboxes with no pipeline or evidence trail
- using stale or inconsistent diligence materials
- promising use of funds without delivery-owner review
- treating practitioner preparation work as approval authority
- using AI-generated funding materials without evidence verification
- failing to capture win/loss lessons

## Practical Starting Point

For a team, function, or organization with weak funding management, start with:

1. Define why funding is needed and which funding sources are acceptable.
2. Create a funding pipeline with owner, type, stage, value, timing, fit, next action, and risk notes.
3. Define approval owners for claims, financials, use of funds, terms, and external communications.
4. Build a basic funding readiness packet: strategy, budget/forecast, evidence, risk, team, milestones, and diligence index.
5. Create an acceptance checklist for restrictions, obligations, terms, reporting, and post-funding ownership.
6. Assign relationship and reporting owners for active funders, investors, lenders, sponsors, or grantors.
7. Record post-funding commitments in a visible register.
8. Review the pipeline and active obligations regularly.

Funding management is not about making fundraising slower. It is about making sure the organization knows what it is asking for, what it is promising, what it is accepting, and who gets to say yes before the money starts rearranging the strategy.
