# People Management

## Purpose

People management is the discipline of helping people do useful work, grow capability, meet clear expectations, and remain part of a healthy team without pretending that employment is either a family, a therapy group, or a machine made of feelings.

It covers the day-to-day responsibilities managers have toward individual people: role clarity, expectations, coaching, feedback, performance, development, hiring, onboarding, retention, conflict, fairness, and humane accountability.

People management appears at every leadership level:

- frontline managers manage individuals directly
- managers of managers coach the people-management quality of other managers
- directors and senior leaders design the operating conditions that make good people management possible
- executives and founders set the cultural defaults for how people are treated, rewarded, developed, exited, and trusted

## Core Responsibilities

People managers are responsible for:

- defining role expectations and success measures
- setting priorities and explaining tradeoffs
- building trust through consistency and follow-through
- creating conditions where disagreement and bad news can surface early
- giving specific, timely, behaviour-based feedback
- coaching people toward stronger judgment and capability
- identifying performance issues early
- handling underperformance fairly and clearly
- supporting career development without inventing fake promotion promises
- hiring with structured, role-relevant assessment
- onboarding people into the work, team, context, and norms
- managing conflict before it becomes folklore
- supporting retention through meaningful work, fairness, growth, flexibility, and sane operating habits
- documenting important decisions, expectations, commitments, and risks
- escalating people, legal, safety, health, harassment, discrimination, or ethical concerns appropriately

## What Good Looks Like

Good people management is visible in ordinary behaviour:

- people know what is expected of them
- feedback is not a surprise event reserved for review season
- disagreement can happen before decisions
- commitments are tracked and followed up
- performance concerns are named early and specifically
- development conversations connect to real work
- praise is specific enough to teach what good looks like
- managers do not hoard context as a power source
- people can ask for help without being treated as defective
- accountability applies upward as well as downward

A good people manager is not necessarily beloved every minute. The standard is trust, fairness, clarity, development, and results — not universal emotional approval.

## Key Practices

### Define Role Expectations Clearly

Every person should understand:

- what they own
- what decisions they can make
- what good performance looks like
- what priorities matter most
- what tradeoffs are acceptable
- when to escalate
- how their work connects to team and organizational outcomes

Ambiguity is not empowerment. It is often just unmanaged delegation wearing better shoes.

### Use Regular 1:1s Well

1:1s are not status meetings with softer lighting.

Use them for:

- priorities and blockers
- feedback and coaching
- context sharing
- career development
- decision support
- workload and sustainability
- relationship repair when needed

A useful 1:1 creates clarity or trust. If it creates neither, redesign it.

### Give Specific Feedback Early

Feedback should be:

- specific
- timely
- connected to impact
- focused on behaviour, judgment, output, or collaboration
- clear about whether it is coaching, concern, praise, or a formal expectation

Weak feedback sounds like:

> Be more strategic.

Useful feedback sounds like:

> In the planning meeting, you listed implementation tasks before clarifying the customer outcome. Next time, start by naming the decision we need and the tradeoffs. That will help the team avoid optimizing the wrong work.

Amara, a newly promoted manager, can make praise just as specific: "Your design review surfaced the quiet objection before we committed; keep doing that, and next time send the decision summary within a day." That is more useful than telling someone they showed "good leadership energy," a phrase that should be escorted from the building.

### Coach Through Real Work

Coaching should connect to actual decisions, artifacts, meetings, delivery, conflict, and judgment.

Useful coaching questions:

- What outcome are you trying to create?
- What options have you considered?
- What risk are you avoiding naming?
- Who needs to be consulted before this decision?
- What would make this decision reversible?
- What evidence would change your mind?
- What support do you need, and what do you own?

### Manage Performance Honestly and Humanely

Performance management should not begin with a formal process. It begins with clarity.

Good performance management:

- defines expectations before judging failure
- separates skill gaps, will gaps, context gaps, and health/capacity constraints
- provides support where support is appropriate
- documents patterns and commitments
- sets review points
- escalates when risk, fairness, or legal/formal process requires it
- avoids both cruelty and cowardice

Do not let kindness become avoidance. Do not let accountability become punishment theatre.

### Develop People Deliberately

Development should be tied to real capability, not motivational fog.

Use:

- stretch assignments
- delegated decisions
- mentoring and sponsorship
- feedback on artifacts and meetings
- exposure to customers/stakeholders
- post-decision reviews
- skill-building plans
- succession/deputy opportunities

Avoid promising promotion as a substitute for development. Promotion is an organizational decision; development is a management responsibility.

### Hire With Structured Assessment

Hiring should evaluate role-relevant evidence.

Good practice:

- define the actual role before opening the search
- separate must-have from nice-to-have criteria
- use structured interviews
- ask consistent evidence-seeking questions
- use work samples or practical exercises where appropriate
- assess collaboration and judgment without relying on vague "culture fit"
- document the decision rationale
- broaden sourcing without lowering the evidence bar

### Onboard for Context, Not Just Tools

Onboarding should help a person understand:

- their role
- the team's purpose
- current priorities
- operating norms
- decision rights
- important relationships
- where documentation lives
- how to escalate
- what good looks like in the first 30/60/90 days

A new hire should not have to reverse-engineer the organization through calendar archaeology.

### Handle Conflict Early

People managers should not wait for conflict to become a permanent weather system.

Good conflict management:

- names the issue specifically
- separates facts, interpretations, and feelings
- clarifies the decision or behaviour at stake
- protects respectful disagreement
- interrupts contempt, avoidance, and triangulation
- creates follow-up commitments

## Hiring, Equity, and Pipeline Development

Candidate-pool asymmetry often forms long before a specific hiring decision. Culture, family expectations, education, peer norms, role models, early encouragement, access, mentorship, and self-selection can all affect who can realistically imagine, prepare for, enter, and remain in a field.

Responsible managers should keep three related responsibilities distinct:

- **Pipeline development** expands early exposure, preparation, mentorship, access, and opportunity.
- **Hiring fairness** evaluates actual candidates transparently against role-relevant criteria.
- **Retention and culture** help people who enter a field stay, grow, contribute, and succeed.

A constructive management position is:

- remove artificial filters and biased evaluation practices
- broaden candidate sourcing without lowering the evidence bar
- use structured interviews, work samples, and role-relevant selection criteria
- invest in early exposure, mentoring, sponsorship, scholarships, internships, apprenticeships, and returnship pathways where the organization can have real influence
- support retention through fair management, flexible practices where possible, psychological safety, sponsorship, and respectful team norms
- review hiring outcomes for patterns without reducing individual candidates to demographic symbols
- be honest about which interventions belong before hiring, during hiring, and after hiring

This approach is pro-fairness, pro-opportunity, and pro-role-relevant assessment. One hiring manager cannot repair every upstream access gap at the point of selection, but managers are still responsible for improving the parts of the system they control: build broader pathways, assess candidates fairly, and create conditions where people can succeed after they join.

## Disagree and Commit

People management decisions often involve real disagreement:

- Is the role expectation clear enough?
- Is the performance issue skill, will, context, workload, health, or management failure?
- Is a promotion case ready?
- Is the hiring decision based on evidence or comfort?
- Is a team member being under-supported or over-protected?
- Is the manager avoiding a hard conversation?

Before a people decision, managers should surface relevant disagreement and evidence. After the decision, the leadership chain should commit to one clear path.

Disagree and commit does not mean hiding ethical, legal, harassment, discrimination, safety, or retaliation concerns. Those must be escalated through the appropriate channel.

For normal management judgment calls, the rule is:

- debate before the decision
- document the rationale where useful
- communicate consistently after the decision
- review outcomes honestly

## Metrics and Signals

People management should not reduce humans to dashboards, but signals matter.

Useful signals include:

- clarity of role expectations
- 1:1 consistency and quality
- feedback timeliness
- performance issue cycle time
- retention and regretted attrition
- internal mobility and development progress
- onboarding time-to-productivity
- hiring process quality and consistency
- employee engagement / pulse themes
- conflict recurrence
- burnout or workload risk
- succession/deputy coverage for critical roles

Metrics should trigger questions, not replace judgment.

## Escalation Rules

Escalate early when:

- harassment, discrimination, retaliation, safety, legal, or ethical concerns appear
- a performance issue may become formal
- health, disability, accommodation, or protected leave may be relevant
- a conflict crosses teams or reporting lines
- a manager lacks authority to resolve the issue
- a key person presents retention, succession, or burnout risk
- a decision may create significant fairness, morale, legal, or reputation risk

Escalation is not failure. Hiding people risk until it becomes institutional folklore is failure.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

In people management, AI tooling will mostly help with drafting role expectations, summarizing feedback themes, finding policy/context, preparing interview or development-plan materials, and spotting patterns in engagement or attrition signals. The practical opportunity is better preparation and consistency, not outsourcing judgment about humans.

Managers must keep people decisions human-owned: hiring, promotion, performance, discipline, accommodation, and termination require accountable human review, evidence checking, bias awareness, and publication/privacy sensitivity. AI can prepare inputs; it should not become a quiet personnel decision-maker with a friendly interface.

## Anti-Patterns

- avoiding hard conversations and calling it empathy
- surprising people with feedback during review season
- delegating accountability without authority
- treating 1:1s as status extraction
- confusing popularity with trust
- using vague "culture fit" instead of role-relevant evidence
- hiring for comfort and calling it judgment
- letting high performers behave badly because they deliver
- overprotecting underperformance until the rest of the team pays for it
- treating development as promotion theatre
- making promises the organization cannot keep
- hoarding context as managerial leverage
- letting conflict move into side channels
- documenting only when preparing to punish someone
- treating people metrics as truth instead of signals
