# Vendor and Partner Management

<!-- doctrine:id=vendor-and-partner-management -->

## Purpose

Vendor and partner management is the discipline of managing external organizations after selection: suppliers, service providers, contractors, strategic partners, channels, integrators, platforms, data providers, outsourced teams, and ecosystem relationships.

Procurement decides what to buy and under what commercial/control path. Vendor and partner management ensures the relationship, performance, obligations, risks, renewals, value, and exit path are actively managed after the signature ink has dried and begun quietly judging everyone.

Good vendor and partner management helps an organization:

- assign clear ownership for external relationships
- manage vendor and partner performance against expectations
- track obligations, deliverables, service levels, renewals, and commitments
- govern third-party risk, including security, privacy, compliance, operational, financial, and reputational exposure
- maintain escalation paths and relationship cadence
- review value and fit over time
- make renewal, renegotiation, replacement, or exit decisions deliberately
- prevent external dependencies from becoming invisible single points of failure

Outsourcing work does not outsource accountability. It merely adds more contracts to the blast radius.

## Core Responsibilities

Vendor and partner management is responsible for ensuring the organization can:

- identify vendors, partners, suppliers, and external dependencies
- classify relationships by criticality, risk, value, and dependency
- assign business, technical, operational, commercial, and risk owners
- maintain current contract, renewal, support, and escalation information
- review performance, service quality, relationship health, and delivered value
- track obligations and commitments in both directions
- manage security, privacy, compliance, legal, financial, and operational risks
- handle incidents, disputes, missed obligations, and escalations
- govern changes, subprocessor updates, pricing changes, roadmap changes, and service changes
- make renewal, renegotiation, replacement, and exit decisions before auto-renewal panic week
- coordinate across procurement, finance, legal, security, compliance, operations, product, support, and business owners

The organization should know who owns each relationship and what would happen if that external party failed, changed terms, got acquired, suffered a breach, missed delivery, or simply became terrible in a slow expensive way.

## Vendor vs Partner

Use the distinction deliberately.

A **vendor** primarily provides goods or services under contract.

Examples:

- SaaS provider
- cloud or hosting provider
- outsourced support provider
- payroll processor
- managed security provider
- software development agency
- equipment supplier
- data provider

A **partner** contributes to mutual strategy, market access, delivery, integration, ecosystem value, or customer outcomes.

Examples:

- channel partner
- implementation partner
- strategic technology partner
- co-selling partner
- integration ecosystem partner
- delivery alliance
- community or open-source partner where mutual obligations exist

Some relationships are both. The label matters less than the management model: performance, risk, obligations, governance, value, and exit path must be clear.

## Relationship Inventory

Maintain a current inventory of material vendors and partners.

Capture:

- vendor / partner name
- service or relationship purpose
- business owner
- technical / operational owner
- contract owner
- risk owner
- criticality
- data / system access
- contract term and renewal date
- spend and budget owner
- support path and escalation contacts
- performance review cadence
- risk review cadence
- exit / replacement notes

The inventory should include active, pending, renewing, paused, and exiting relationships. Invisible vendors are where risk goes to compost.

## Classification and Criticality

Classify relationships by:

- business criticality
- customer impact
- operational dependency
- data sensitivity
- system access
- financial exposure
- regulatory/compliance relevance
- switching difficulty
- concentration risk
- reputational impact
- strategic importance

A low-spend vendor can be high criticality if it touches production, customer data, payroll, payments, identity, compliance evidence, or core operations. A huge contract can be low strategic value if it mainly funds a very expensive habit.

## Ownership Model

Every material vendor or partner should have named owners.

Common roles:

- **Business owner** — owns business value, fit, and renewal recommendation
- **Contract owner** — owns contract record, terms, notice dates, and commercial obligations
- **Operational owner** — owns day-to-day service performance and support interface
- **Technical owner** — owns integrations, architecture, access, configuration, and technical dependencies
- **Risk owner** — owns security, privacy, compliance, legal, operational, or financial risk coordination
- **Executive sponsor** — owns strategic or high-risk relationship escalation where needed

Small organizations may combine roles. Combining roles is acceptable. Having no owner because “the vendor handles it” is how adults create haunted spreadsheets.

## Vendor Lifecycle

Manage the full lifecycle:

1. identify need / relationship purpose
2. select and contract through procurement
3. onboard and integrate
4. operate and review performance
5. manage obligations, changes, incidents, and risk
6. review value and relationship health
7. renew, renegotiate, expand, replace, or exit
8. offboard, archive evidence, and transfer ownership or data as needed

Do not treat signature as the finish line. Signature is the point at which the relationship becomes real and starts accumulating consequences.

## Onboarding and Integration

Vendor/partner onboarding should confirm:

- contract and statement of work are available
- owners and contacts are named
- success criteria and service expectations are clear
- access, identity, data, and security controls are approved
- implementation / integration plan exists
- support and escalation paths are documented
- billing and invoice process is known
- reporting cadence is scheduled
- operational runbooks or admin procedures are available
- exit or fallback considerations are recorded where relevant

Onboarding is especially important when the relationship affects customers, regulated data, production systems, employee data, financial processes, or core operations.

## Performance Management

Review vendor and partner performance against expectations.

Performance signals include:

- service-level performance
- quality of deliverables
- support responsiveness
- incident frequency and handling
- delivery against milestones
- customer/user impact
- cost vs value
- relationship responsiveness
- roadmap alignment
- compliance with obligations
- audit/security evidence freshness
- issue resolution and corrective actions

A performance review should produce decisions or actions, not just a ritual where everyone says “partnership” until the call ends.

## Quarterly / Periodic Business Reviews

Use periodic reviews for material relationships.

A useful review covers:

- business value delivered
- performance against service levels or expectations
- open issues and corrective actions
- incidents, escalations, and lessons learned
- usage/adoption and cost trends
- upcoming renewals, pricing changes, or contract obligations
- security, privacy, compliance, and risk updates
- roadmap or service changes
- customer or operational feedback
- decisions needed

For strategic partners, also review joint goals, pipeline or delivery health, market/customer feedback, governance quality, and mutual commitments.

## Obligation and Commitment Management

Track obligations in both directions.

Vendor/partner obligations may include:

- deliverables
- service levels
- support response times
- security controls
- audit evidence
- breach notification
- data processing restrictions
- subprocessor notice
- insurance requirements
- regulatory support
- implementation milestones
- roadmap or integration commitments
- renewal / cancellation notice terms
- exit assistance

Internal obligations may include:

- payment
- access provisioning
- customer inputs
- implementation resources
- usage minimums
- notice periods
- data handling responsibilities
- timely review or acceptance
- escalation participation

An obligation nobody tracks is not really governed. It is a future dispute in larval form.

## Third-Party Risk Management

Third-party risk can include:

- security risk
- privacy / data protection risk
- compliance or regulatory risk
- operational resilience risk
- business continuity risk
- financial stability risk
- legal / contract risk
- reputational risk
- concentration risk
- geopolitical / jurisdictional risk
- subprocessor / subcontractor risk
- AI/model/provider risk where applicable
- supply-chain and dependency risk

Risk review should be proportionate. A lunch vendor does not need the same review as an identity provider. If they do, your process has become a small paperwork monarchy.

High-risk vendors should have:

- named risk owner
- current risk assessment
- required evidence and review cadence
- accepted risks with expiry/review dates
- incident notification and escalation path
- continuity/fallback plan
- exit strategy

## Security, Privacy, and Compliance Evidence

For vendors with sensitive access or regulated obligations, maintain evidence such as:

- SOC 2 / ISO 27001 / ISO 42001 or equivalent reports where relevant
- penetration test summaries or security attestations
- data processing agreement
- subprocessor list and change process
- breach notification terms
- access-control model
- encryption and retention commitments
- audit rights or evidence package
- compliance mappings
- business continuity / disaster recovery evidence
- vulnerability disclosure or security contact

Evidence should be current enough to support the risk decision. A four-year-old PDF in a shared drive is less “assurance” and more “artifact fossil.”

## Change and Subprocessor Management

Vendors and partners change.

Track material changes such as:

- pricing changes
- contract term changes
- service-level changes
- product or roadmap changes
- support model changes
- data location changes
- subprocessor or subcontractor changes
- ownership/acquisition changes
- security/compliance posture changes
- API/integration changes
- end-of-life / deprecation notices

Define who reviews changes, what triggers escalation, and how objections or risk acceptances are recorded.

## Incidents and Escalation

Vendor/partner incidents may include:

- missed service levels
- data/security incidents
- prolonged outage
- failed delivery milestone
- support breakdown
- contract dispute
- billing dispute
- customer-impacting defect
- compliance evidence failure
- relationship breakdown

Escalations should include:

- issue summary
- customer/business impact
- contract or obligation reference
- evidence
- requested remedy or decision
- internal owner
- vendor/partner owner
- deadline
- escalation path

Escalation should be firm, factual, and documented. Dramatic forwarding chains are not a governance mechanism; they are just email trying to become weather.

## Renewal and Exit Decisions

Renewal should be a decision, not an accident caused by a missed notice date.

Before renewal, review:

- business need and value
- performance history
- usage/adoption
- cost and price change
- contract terms
- open issues and escalations
- risk posture and current evidence
- alternatives
- integration/switching cost
- exit feasibility
- owner recommendation

Possible decisions:

- renew as-is
- renew with changed terms
- expand
- reduce scope
- renegotiate
- replace
- exit / terminate
- short extension while replacement is prepared

Exit planning should cover data export, deletion/retention, access removal, customer or operational transition, contract notice, replacement timing, knowledge transfer, final invoices, and evidence archiving.

## Partner Governance

Strategic partners need governance beyond supplier performance.

Define:

- shared goals
- mutual commitments
- executive sponsors
- operating cadence
- pipeline or delivery review
- customer ownership boundaries
- brand / messaging controls
- data and confidentiality rules
- conflict resolution path
- joint risk and compliance obligations
- exit or wind-down process

Partner governance should protect trust without confusing partnership with unlimited obligation. A partner is not a magical exception to decision rights, risk controls, or economics.

## Cross-Functional Governance

Vendor and partner management usually crosses functions.

Common participants:

- business owner
- procurement
- finance
- legal
- security
- privacy
- compliance
- operations
- architecture / IT
- product
- support / customer success
- risk management
- executive sponsor

Governance should define when each function participates. Dragging every function into every vendor review is wasteful. Excluding the right function from a material decision is how risk writes its memoir.

## Value and Relationship Health

Review value and relationship health separately.

Value asks:

- Are we getting the expected outcome?
- Is usage/adoption appropriate?
- Is cost justified?
- Has the business need changed?
- Is this still the right solution?

Relationship health asks:

- Are communication and escalation working?
- Are commitments honored?
- Is trust improving or degrading?
- Are issues resolved promptly?
- Are both sides transparent about changes?

A vendor can be high value but hard to work with. A partner can be pleasant but strategically useless. Both facts matter.

## Level-Specific Responsibilities

### Team Leads and Frontline Managers

- report service issues, support breakdowns, usage gaps, and operational friction
- follow approved vendor/partner processes
- avoid creating unofficial commitments or side deals
- provide evidence for performance reviews and renewal decisions

### Managers of Managers

- coordinate vendor/partner impact across teams
- resolve shared ownership gaps
- ensure operational issues are escalated and tracked
- compare relationship value across related teams or services

### Directors and Senior Leaders

- own material vendor/partner relationships and renewal recommendations
- make tradeoffs between cost, risk, performance, and strategic value
- ensure cross-functional governance is proportionate and effective
- approve escalations, replacements, or exits within authority

### Executives and Founders

- own strategic partner relationships and high-risk vendor decisions
- set risk appetite and commercial guardrails
- prevent executive side deals from bypassing controls
- decide major renewal, exit, concentration, or strategic-dependency tradeoffs

## Disagree and Commit

Vendor and partner decisions need honest disagreement before commitment.

People should be able to say:

- this vendor is cheaper because risk is being ignored
- this partner is strategically attractive but operationally weak
- renewal is being driven by inertia, not value
- the exit path is not realistic
- subprocessor or data exposure changes need review
- the vendor’s performance record does not support expansion
- a relationship owner is too close to the vendor to challenge poor performance
- a customer commitment depends on a vendor obligation that is not controlled
- the contract creates risk the business owner does not understand

After a decision is made, teams should commit to the chosen path and controls. If the organization accepts a vendor risk, it should own that decision explicitly rather than pretending the risk lives in procurement’s filing cabinet.

Artifacts should help the organization manage real dependencies. If they exist only for audit week and are stale by the next renewal, they have joined the paperwork undead.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

In vendor and partner management, AI tooling will monitor obligations, summarize performance, detect renewal risks, compare support history, track security/compliance signals, and draft QBR or escalation materials.

Practical uses include:

- summarizing contract obligations and notice periods
- identifying renewals without current owner recommendations
- comparing service tickets, incidents, and SLA performance over time
- drafting vendor performance or partner business review packs
- flagging missing security, privacy, compliance, or continuity evidence
- detecting subprocessor or service-change notices needing review
- extracting commitments from meeting notes and support threads
- drafting escalation briefs with evidence and decision asks

The risk is outsourced visibility theatre: dashboards look current while obligations, subprocessor changes, or relationship risks are missed. Managers still own vendor accountability, escalation, renewal decisions, risk acceptance, and partner trust.

AI-generated vendor summaries should cite source records, identify missing evidence, separate contractual facts from inferred sentiment, and avoid smoothing over unresolved issues. A model-generated QBR that says “partnership remains strong” while support tickets are on fire is not analysis. It is a scented candle in a server room.

## Anti-Patterns

Watch for these failure modes:

- treating signature as the end of vendor management
- having no named business owner after purchase
- discovering renewals after the notice date passes
- managing vendors only through procurement, with no operational owner
- ignoring security, privacy, compliance, or continuity evidence after onboarding
- confusing a friendly account manager with strong vendor performance
- letting strategic partners bypass risk and financial controls
- tolerating chronic underperformance because switching is annoying
- accepting subprocessor or service changes without review
- expanding a vendor relationship while open issues remain unresolved
- tracking spend without tracking value
- relying on one vendor for critical capability without fallback or exit plan
- letting relationship owners make side commitments
- conducting QBRs that produce no decisions or corrective actions
- using AI summaries that hide missing evidence, stale obligations, or unresolved incidents

## Practical Starting Point

For a small or growing organization, start with:

1. Build a vendor/partner inventory for all material external relationships.
2. Assign business, contract, operational, technical, and risk owners where relevant.
3. Record renewal dates, notice periods, spend, criticality, data/system access, and support paths.
4. Classify relationships by criticality and third-party risk.
5. Review high-risk and high-value relationships on a cadence.
6. Track obligations, incidents, escalations, and corrective actions.
7. Make renewal decisions before notice dates, with performance and risk evidence.
8. Define exit/offboarding steps for critical vendors and strategic partners.

The goal is not to mistrust every external party. The goal is to make external dependency visible enough that trust, performance, risk, cost, and accountability can all survive contact with reality.
