# Research Management

<!-- doctrine:id=research-management -->

## Purpose

Research management is the discipline of directing, governing, resourcing, reviewing, and learning from research activities so the organization can make better decisions from credible evidence.

It covers how research questions are chosen, how research work is prioritized, how evidence standards are set, how ethics and compliance boundaries are handled, how research outputs are reviewed, and how findings move into product, strategy, policy, operations, funding, publication, or investment decisions.

Research management is not the same as doing the research. Research practitioners run studies, literature reviews, interviews, experiments, fieldwork, analysis, synthesis, and evidence production. Research management defines the operating system around that work: priorities, quality bars, approval gates, funding interfaces, stakeholder alignment, risk controls, and decision use.

Good research management helps an organization:

- ask the right questions before spending time on answers
- connect research work to strategy, customer/user needs, science, policy, operations, or investment decisions
- prioritize research by decision value, uncertainty, feasibility, risk, and timing
- protect evidence quality, reproducibility, participant welfare, data integrity, and ethical boundaries
- avoid turning research into decorative validation theatre
- make funding, publication, communication, and intellectual-property decisions deliberately
- capture knowledge so findings remain useful after the project, presentation, or heroic spreadsheet has aged into folklore

Research is expensive when done well and dangerous when done badly. Research management exists because confidence is not evidence, and a beautifully formatted deck can still be nonsense in sensible shoes.

## Scope

Research management may cover:

- research strategy and agenda setting
- research portfolio and prioritization
- research question framing
- study design review and method fit
- research governance, ethics, privacy, legal, and compliance boundaries
- evidence standards and quality review
- participant/customer/community safeguards where relevant
- research data ownership, access, retention, and reproducibility
- research operations, tooling, vendors, panels, labs, fieldwork, or repositories
- funding interfaces, grant/investment constraints, and sponsor expectations
- publication, communication, claims, and disclosure gates
- intellectual property and commercialization interfaces
- research-to-decision handoffs
- knowledge capture, lessons learned, and reuse
- research metrics and portfolio health
- AI-assisted research controls and limitations

The discipline applies across academic research, product research, market research, policy research, technical research, scientific research, clinical or health-adjacent research, security research, operational research, and internal organizational research. The standards vary by context. The need for explicit management does not.

## Research Program Ownership

Research needs named ownership.

Clarify:

- who owns the research agenda
- who owns prioritization across research requests
- who owns method and evidence standards
- who owns ethics, privacy, legal, compliance, and participant-safety interfaces
- who owns research data and repositories
- who owns publication or external communication approval
- who owns funding/sponsor obligations
- who owns research operations, tooling, vendors, or panels
- who decides whether findings are strong enough to support a decision

In small organizations, one person may manage several of these areas. That can work if accountability is explicit. It does not work if research is "owned by everyone" until the findings become inconvenient, at which point it is suddenly owned by the nearest analyst holding a chart.

## Research Strategy and Agenda

Research strategy should explain what the organization needs to learn and why.

Define:

- strategic questions the organization needs answered
- decision areas where evidence is weak or contested
- major uncertainty, risk, or opportunity themes
- research domains and owners
- expected decision uses
- time horizon: immediate, quarterly, annual, multi-year
- required evidence quality by decision type
- funding or resource constraints
- stakeholders and decision forums
- what research should not be pursued now

A useful research agenda is not a list of interesting questions. It is a managed set of learning priorities tied to decisions. Curiosity is good. Unbounded curiosity with a budget line is how organizations discover expensive trivia.

## Research Portfolio and Prioritization

Research demand usually exceeds capacity.

Prioritize research using factors such as:

- decision importance
- uncertainty reduction
- timing and decision deadline
- risk if the organization acts without evidence
- feasibility and cost
- ethical or compliance constraints
- stakeholder dependency
- funding or sponsor obligation
- reuse value across teams or decisions
- whether existing evidence already answers the question well enough

Common prioritization categories:

- critical decision support
- exploratory learning
- validation or risk reduction
- compliance or required evidence
- publication or external credibility
- funding/investment readiness
- operational improvement
- backlog or watchlist

Avoid treating every request as equal. Also avoid treating the loudest stakeholder as the research strategy. That is not prioritization; it is acoustics.

## Research Question Framing

Good research starts with a clear question.

Before approving research work, clarify:

- what decision the research should inform
- what is already known
- what assumptions are being tested
- what evidence would change the decision
- what method is appropriate
- what population, system, market, process, or evidence base is in scope
- what constraints affect method choice
- what ethical, privacy, legal, or safety review is needed
- what "good enough" evidence means for this decision

If no possible finding would change the decision, the work may be communication, justification, or stakeholder theatre rather than research. That can still be useful in rare cases, but it should not be mislabeled as inquiry.

## Method Fit and Study Design Review

Research management should ensure method fit without micromanaging practitioner craft.

Review whether the proposed approach matches the question:

- qualitative methods for meaning, motivation, experience, failure modes, and context
- quantitative methods for measurement, prevalence, comparison, forecasting, and trend
- experiments for causal inference where feasible and ethical
- literature/source review for existing knowledge and prior art
- prototype or concept testing for response to possible solutions
- field observation for real workflow and environmental context
- technical investigation for feasibility, performance, reliability, or security behaviour
- market or competitive research for positioning, demand, and ecosystem evidence

Ask whether the design has:

- clear research objectives
- appropriate sample, corpus, population, or evidence source
- bias and limitation handling
- analysis plan
- ethical/privacy safeguards
- data-handling plan
- quality/review checkpoints
- expected outputs and decision use

Managers do not need to perform every method themselves. They do need enough method literacy to avoid approving a survey when the real problem is unclear, or approving five interviews as proof of market demand because the quotes looked confident.

## Evidence Standards

Research management defines the evidence bar for different decisions.

Evidence standards may vary by:

- impact of the decision
- reversibility
- safety, legal, privacy, financial, or reputational risk
- customer/user impact
- public or external claims
- funding, investment, or grant reliance
- operational dependency
- uncertainty and novelty

Useful evidence review checks:

- source credibility
- method appropriateness
- sample or corpus adequacy
- data quality and provenance
- analysis transparency
- uncertainty and limitations
- conflicts of interest or sponsor influence
- reproducibility or audit trail where needed
- whether findings actually support the claimed decision

Evidence should be strong enough for the decision it supports. A low-risk design tweak may need lightweight evidence. A clinical, financial, regulatory, investment, safety, or public-claim decision needs a much higher bar. The same sticky note should not serve both jobs.

## Ethics, Privacy, Legal, and Compliance Boundaries

Research can create real harm.

Research management should define when review is required for:

- human participants
- personal, sensitive, regulated, or confidential data
- vulnerable populations
- deception, pressure, incentives, or power imbalance
- health, safety, employment, financial, educational, or legal impact
- recording, observation, tracking, or telemetry
- customer or community engagement
- cross-border data handling
- third-party research vendors
- publication or public communication
- AI-assisted data processing or synthesis

The exact review process depends on context. Some work may require formal ethics review, legal review, privacy impact assessment, compliance review, customer approval, institutional review, or sponsor approval. Some may only need a lightweight documented check.

Do not route around ethics or privacy because the work is "only discovery." Discovery is exactly where organizations are tempted to collect too much, promise too vaguely, and call the whole thing learning.

## Research Operations

Research needs operational support.

Research operations may include:

- intake and prioritization workflow
- participant/panel/vendor management
- consent and disclosure materials
- scheduling and fieldwork logistics
- research repositories
- data storage and access controls
- tooling, labs, devices, environments, and licenses
- templates and playbooks
- transcription, coding, analysis, and synthesis support
- evidence packet standards
- publication and archive processes
- research metrics and reporting

Good research operations make quality easier. Bad research operations make every study a handcrafted panic object.

## Funding and Sponsor Interfaces

Research often depends on funding, grants, investment, sponsorship, or internal budget.

Research management should clarify:

- funding source and constraints
- sponsor expectations
- allowable costs
- reporting obligations
- decision rights
- independence and conflict-of-interest boundaries
- milestone or deliverable commitments
- publication or disclosure obligations
- intellectual-property expectations
- what evidence can and cannot be claimed for funding/investment purposes

Funding pressure must not distort findings. A sponsor can fund research. A sponsor should not get to buy a conclusion and receive methodology as gift wrap.

Use Funding Management for the management system around acquiring funding or investment. Use Research Management for the integrity, portfolio, governance, and decision use of the research itself. In practice these disciplines touch often, which is why the boundary needs names instead of vibes.

## Research Outputs and Decision Handoffs

Research should produce outputs that decision-makers can use.

Common outputs:

- research brief or plan
- study protocol or design review
- literature/source review
- interview or observation synthesis
- experiment or test report
- data analysis notebook or evidence packet
- insight report
- recommendation memo
- risk/limitation note
- decision record
- publication or communication draft
- knowledge-base entry

Research outputs should state:

- question
- method
- evidence sources
- key findings
- confidence and limitations
- assumptions
- risks
- decision implications
- recommended next steps
- what the research does not prove

The handoff matters. Research findings should not become automatic decisions by sliding into a persuasive deck. Decision owners still own tradeoffs, commitments, funding, timing, and risk acceptance.

## Publication, Communication, and Claims

Research findings can affect reputation, customers, investors, regulators, partners, and employees.

Before external publication or high-stakes internal communication, review:

- whether the claim is supported by evidence
- limitations and uncertainty
- confidentiality, privacy, and consent obligations
- sponsor/funder disclosure
- intellectual property and publication rights
- legal/regulatory implications
- brand and reputational risk
- whether language overstates causality, generality, novelty, or certainty

Good communication makes findings understandable without laundering uncertainty out of existence. Bad communication turns research into marketing cosplay with footnotes.

## Research Data, Repositories, and Reproducibility

Research data needs governance.

Define:

- source data and collection method
- consent and permitted use
- storage location
- access rights
- sensitivity/classification
- retention and deletion
- anonymization or de-identification where relevant
- analysis scripts, notebooks, or workflows
- versioning and provenance
- reproducibility expectations
- archive and reuse rules

Not every research activity needs academic-grade reproducibility. But high-stakes or reusable research should leave enough trail that another competent person can understand what was done, what evidence was used, and why the conclusion did not fall out of the sky.

## Knowledge Capture and Reuse

Research should improve organizational memory.

Capture:

- final outputs
- reusable evidence
- decision records
- limitations and failed hypotheses
- participant/customer/community signals
- methods that worked or failed
- unresolved questions
- follow-up research needs
- related artifacts and source links

Connect research outputs to Knowledge Management so future teams can find and trust them. Otherwise the organization repeats studies, rediscovers old constraints, and declares each rediscovery innovative because nobody found the previous PDF.

## Collaboration Interfaces

Research management often interfaces with:

- product management
- strategy and portfolio management
- funding management
- data and analytics management
- knowledge management
- architecture and technical research
- security, privacy, legal, compliance, and risk management
- customer, support, sales, and marketing management
- operations management
- people management and HR research
- vendors, partners, academia, community groups, or public agencies

Clarify handoffs, decision rights, and evidence needs. Research can inform many decisions. It should not silently own all of them.

## Research Practitioner Boundary

For detailed practitioner scope, see [Research Practitioner](https://github.com/RusDavies/docs-practitioner-practices/blob/master/roles/research-practitioner.md).


Research practitioners perform the hands-on work:

- research planning details
- source/literature review
- study, interview, observation, experiment, or fieldwork execution
- data collection and cleaning
- coding, analysis, synthesis, and interpretation
- research artifact preparation
- reproducibility and evidence packet assembly

Research management owns the management frame:

- why this work matters
- how it is prioritized and funded
- what standards and approvals apply
- what decisions it may inform
- who can approve action from findings
- how knowledge is captured and reused

A manager may also be a researcher in a small organization, but the active role should be explicit. "I can do both" is not the same as "I can approve my own evidence, funding claim, publication, and risk acceptance." Convenient, yes. Sound governance, no.

## Metrics and Review Cadence

Useful research management measures include:

- research backlog health
- decision coverage for major uncertainty areas
- time from question intake to usable finding
- research capacity and bottlenecks
- evidence quality/review outcomes
- reuse of prior research
- stakeholder satisfaction with decision usefulness
- ethics/privacy/compliance review timeliness
- publication or claim review defects
- number of duplicated studies avoided
- unresolved high-risk unknowns

Do not measure research only by volume: number of studies, interviews, reports, or pages. Output volume can rise while decision quality quietly collapses into a swamp wearing slide numbers.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

AI tools can help with source discovery, literature mapping, interview guide drafting, coding assistance, synthesis support, transcript summarization, evidence clustering, prior-work retrieval, and research repository search.

AI tools also increase risk:

- fabricated citations or sources
- overconfident synthesis
- hidden privacy/data exposure
- biased coding or summarization
- unsupported generalization
- loss of provenance
- accidental reuse of sensitive research material
- sponsor-friendly wording that outruns evidence

Research management should define which AI uses are allowed, what human review is required, what data may be processed, how provenance is preserved, and when AI output is treated as a working aid rather than evidence.

AI can help process research. It cannot make weak evidence strong by sounding calm in paragraphs.

## Anti-Patterns

Watch for these failure modes:

- starting research without a decision or learning objective
- treating stakeholder preference as evidence
- choosing the method before clarifying the question
- using research to validate a decision already made
- overgeneralizing from small or biased samples
- hiding limitations because they make the story less tidy
- allowing funding or sponsor pressure to shape conclusions
- skipping ethics/privacy/legal review because the work feels informal
- confusing research output with decision authority
- letting research repositories become unowned archives
- publishing claims stronger than the evidence supports
- repeating research because prior findings were not captured or findable
- using AI summaries without source verification or provenance
- measuring research activity instead of decision usefulness

## Practical Starting Point

For a team, function, or organization with weak research management, start with:

1. Name the decisions or uncertainties research should support.
2. Create a visible research backlog with owner, question, decision use, priority, and timing.
3. Define lightweight evidence standards by decision risk.
4. Create an ethics/privacy/compliance review trigger checklist.
5. Decide where research outputs, data, limitations, and decision records live.
6. Identify active funding, sponsor, publication, or external-claim constraints.
7. Separate research-practitioner execution from management approval.
8. Review the portfolio monthly or quarterly for decision value, duplication, capacity, and unresolved high-risk unknowns.

Research management is not about making inquiry bureaucratic. It is about making learning deliberate enough that evidence can survive contact with budgets, deadlines, politics, and PowerPoint.
