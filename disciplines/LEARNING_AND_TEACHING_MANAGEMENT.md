# Learning and Teaching Management

<!-- doctrine:id=learning-and-teaching-management -->

## Purpose

Learning and teaching management is the discipline of building, governing, improving, and measuring the systems that help people develop capability.

It covers how learning needs are identified, how curricula and teaching methods are chosen, how instructors and facilitators are supported, how learners are assessed, how capability is certified or gated, and how learning transfers into real work.

Learning and teaching management is not the same as Knowledge Management. Knowledge Management makes knowledge findable, trustworthy, current, and governed. Learning and teaching management helps people build skill, judgment, confidence, and readiness through instruction, practice, feedback, coaching, assessment, and reinforcement.

It is also not the same as People Management. People managers own role expectations, feedback, development context, staffing, performance, and humane accountability. Learning and teaching management owns the capability-building system that may support many managers, teams, customers, partners, students, or communities.

Good learning and teaching management helps an organization:

- identify capability gaps before they become performance, quality, safety, or customer problems
- build role-relevant learning paths
- design teaching that changes behaviour, not just attendance records
- support instructors, facilitators, trainers, coaches, mentors, and curriculum designers
- assess readiness with evidence instead of cheerful vibes
- connect onboarding, enablement, change adoption, compliance training, and professional development
- make learning accessible, inclusive, and usable by different learners
- measure demonstrated capability rather than worshipping completion dashboards
- use AI tutoring and content-generation tools without turning hallucination into curriculum

Training is not automatically learning. A person can complete a module, pass a quiz, receive a badge, and still be dangerously unprepared. Computers have achieved many wonders, including letting us measure the wrong thing at industrial scale.

## Scope

Learning and teaching management may cover:

- learning strategy and capability planning
- capability-gap analysis
- curriculum and program design
- role-based learning paths
- onboarding and enablement programs
- instruction, facilitation, and teaching-quality standards
- coaching, mentoring, apprenticeship, and peer-learning interfaces
- learner assessment, readiness gates, credentials, and certification
- learning operations, scheduling, cohorts, tooling, content systems, and instructor support
- learning content lifecycle, review, ownership, and retirement
- accessibility, inclusion, learner safety, and support
- learning transfer into real work
- training for organizational change, compliance, systems, products, processes, customers, partners, or communities
- measurement of learning effectiveness and capability outcomes
- AI-assisted tutoring, practice, content drafting, translation, summarization, feedback, and evaluation controls

The discipline applies in companies, schools, universities, community programs, internal enablement teams, customer education, partner training, professional development, open source communities, and any other setting where people are expected to learn something useful rather than merely endure slides.

## Learning System Ownership

Learning systems need named ownership.

Clarify:

- who owns learning strategy
- who owns each curriculum or program
- who owns capability standards by role, level, product, process, or domain
- who owns instructor/facilitator quality
- who owns learner assessment and readiness gates
- who owns learning operations and tooling
- who owns learning content quality, review, and retirement
- who owns accessibility and learner-support standards
- who owns learning data, privacy, and reporting
- who decides whether someone is ready to perform, teach, certify, or advance

Ownership should sit close enough to the work to understand the capability being built and senior enough to make tradeoffs. A central learning function can provide standards and support, but it cannot invent domain credibility by owning the calendar invite.

## Learning Strategy and Capability Gaps

Learning strategy should explain what capability the organization needs and why.

Define:

- strategic capabilities required now and soon
- role, team, customer, partner, student, or community groups in scope
- current capability gaps
- expected behaviours or performance outcomes
- risks if people remain unprepared
- priority learning needs by urgency and impact
- learning methods likely to fit the need
- manager, coach, mentor, instructor, and practitioner roles
- required assessment, credentialing, or readiness gates
- learning investments that should not be pursued now

Not every problem is a training problem. If the real problem is poor tooling, impossible workload, unclear decisions, broken incentives, or leadership inconsistency, adding a course is just decorating the crater.

## Curriculum and Program Design

Curriculum design should connect learning objectives, content, practice, feedback, and assessment.

Useful curriculum design clarifies:

- target learner groups
- prerequisite knowledge or skill
- learning objectives
- key concepts, behaviours, and judgment areas
- required practice and application
- feedback mechanisms
- assessment approach
- sequence and pacing
- expected artifacts or demonstrations
- instructor/facilitator notes
- accessibility and support needs
- content owner and review cadence

A curriculum is not a folder of presentations. It is a deliberately sequenced path from current capability to required capability. If the sequence is "read these 37 documents and good luck," the curriculum designer has left the building.

## Teaching and Facilitation Quality

Teaching quality matters.

Learning and teaching management should define standards for:

- lesson planning
- explanation quality
- examples and demonstrations
- learner engagement
- practice design
- questioning and discussion
- feedback quality
- pacing and cognitive load
- psychological safety and participation norms
- facilitation of disagreement or uncertainty
- adaptation to learner needs
- instructor preparation and support

Good teaching makes the important thing easier to understand and harder to misuse. Bad teaching makes the instructor feel productive while learners quietly build a private mythology of guesses.

Priya, an enablement lead, can improve a product-training cohort by replacing a two-hour feature tour with short demonstrations, realistic customer scenarios, practice calls, peer review, and a readiness rubric. Same product. Less theatre. More actual competence.

## Learning Paths and Role Readiness

Learning paths should show how people progress toward real capability.

A role-based learning path may include:

- orientation and context
- foundational concepts
- tool/system practice
- process and decision-rights training
- compliance, safety, privacy, or security requirements
- supervised practice
- shadowing or pairing
- scenario work
- observed performance
- readiness assessment
- ongoing practice and refreshers
- advanced or specialist development

Learning paths should avoid pretending that one generic course fits every role. A frontline support agent, technical lead, sales engineer, policy analyst, teacher, and executive sponsor may all need to understand the same change, but not in the same way or at the same depth.

## Practice, Coaching, Mentoring, and Apprenticeship

Capability grows through application.

Learning programs should deliberately use:

- worked examples
- scenario practice
- simulations or labs
- role play where appropriate
- shadowing
- pairing
- mentoring
- coaching conversations
- feedback on real artifacts
- after-action reviews
- communities of practice
- progressive responsibility

People Management often owns individual development conversations. Learning and teaching management should provide the shared learning structures, methods, curricula, rubrics, and support that make those conversations useful instead of inspirational fog.

## Assessment, Credentials, and Readiness Gates

Assessment should match the capability being claimed.

Assessment options include:

- knowledge checks
- practical demonstrations
- scenario responses
- work-sample review
- observed performance
- simulation or lab completion
- portfolio evidence
- peer or instructor review
- manager sign-off
- certification or credentialing
- supervised production work

Use lightweight checks for low-risk learning and stronger readiness gates for high-risk work. A quiz may be enough for awareness. It is not enough for safety-critical operations, regulated activity, high-stakes customer commitments, teaching authority, financial promises, security access, clinical/health-adjacent work, or anything where "I watched the video" would sound stupid in a post-incident review.

Assessments should state what they prove and what they do not prove. Passing a written test may show recall. It does not automatically show judgment under pressure.

## Learning Operations

Learning needs operations.

Learning operations may include:

- intake and prioritization of learning requests
- program calendars and cohort management
- instructor and facilitator scheduling
- learning-management systems and content repositories
- learner enrollment, attendance, and completion tracking
- practice environments or sandboxes
- accessibility accommodations
- translation and localization
- feedback collection
- content versioning and review workflows
- learning data and reporting
- vendor, platform, and license management
- budget and capacity planning

Good learning operations make quality repeatable. Bad learning operations make every cohort a bespoke administrative fire drill with nicer fonts.

## Content Ownership and Lifecycle

Learning content needs lifecycle management.

For significant learning content, define:

- owner
- audience
- learning objective
- approval status
- source material
- related policies, procedures, systems, or product versions
- sensitivity and access rules
- last review date
- next review date or trigger
- obsolete/replacement path
- learner feedback channel
- evidence of effectiveness where available

Learning content should connect to Knowledge Management for authoritative source control. Learning content should not become a parallel truth universe with fresher screenshots and worse governance.

## Accessibility, Inclusion, and Learner Support

Learning systems should be usable by real people.

Consider:

- disability access and accommodations
- language, reading level, and localization
- time zones and scheduling constraints
- bandwidth, device, and environment constraints
- psychological safety in practice and discussion
- cultural context and example design
- neurodiversity and different processing needs
- prior knowledge differences
- learner confidence and support channels
- confidentiality for sensitive learner needs

Inclusion in learning is not just a moral accessory. If the program only works for people who already know the material, have perfect schedules, and enjoy being quizzed in public, it is less a learning system and more a confidence filter.

## Learning Transfer and Reinforcement

Learning is only valuable if it changes practice.

Support transfer through:

- manager reinforcement
- job aids and checklists
- practice close to the time of use
- follow-up coaching
- peer support
- office hours
- communities of practice
- work review and feedback
- process/tool updates
- incentives and expectations aligned with the new behaviour
- reminders and refreshers
- retirement of old ways of working where appropriate

If the old behaviour remains easier, safer, faster, or more rewarded, the training will lose. This is not cynicism. It is operations management standing behind the curtain with a wrench.

## Onboarding and Enablement Handoffs

Learning and teaching management often supports onboarding and enablement.

Clarify handoffs with:

- People Management for role expectations, manager support, performance context, development plans, and individual coaching
- Team Management for working agreements, team norms, peer support, and local onboarding
- Change Management for training and enablement tied to organizational change
- Product, Sales, Support, Customer, and Operations Management for product/process/customer enablement
- Knowledge Management for authoritative source material and maintenance
- Compliance, Security, Privacy, Legal, Risk, or Quality Management for required learning and evidence

Onboarding should not be a scavenger hunt. Enablement should not be a broadcast. Both need a managed learning design that helps people do the work.

## Learning Data, Privacy, and Reporting

Learning data can be useful and sensitive.

Define:

- what learning data is collected
- purpose and allowed use
- access rights
- retention and deletion
- learner privacy expectations
- reporting levels: individual, team, program, organization
- limits on using learning data for performance decisions
- evidence needed for compliance or certification
- safeguards for AI-assisted analysis or tutoring data

Completion data is easy to collect and easy to overinterpret. Readiness, behaviour change, quality improvement, reduced error, safer operations, customer outcomes, and stronger judgment are harder to measure but usually closer to the point.

## Metrics and Review Cadence

Useful learning and teaching management measures include:

- capability gaps identified and prioritized
- learning-path coverage by role or domain
- learner completion where completion actually matters
- assessment pass/fail patterns
- readiness-gate outcomes
- instructor/facilitator quality feedback
- learner confidence and support needs
- observed transfer into work
- quality, safety, compliance, support, sales, delivery, or customer indicators affected by learning
- time to productivity for onboarding
- content freshness and review health
- accessibility or participation gaps
- cost and capacity by program

Review cadence should match risk and volatility. High-risk, regulated, customer-facing, safety-related, security-sensitive, or rapidly changing content needs more frequent review than stable background concepts.

Do not let the dashboard become a shrine to course completions. The organization needs capability, not confetti.

## AI Tooling Evolution

Use [AI Tooling Evolution](../AI_TOOLING_EVOLUTION.md) for the shared model.

AI tools can help with curriculum outlines, example generation, quiz drafting, tutoring, translation, learner practice, scenario generation, summarization, coaching prompts, feedback drafts, content search, and gap analysis.

AI tools also increase risk:

- hallucinated or outdated content
- unsupported claims in generated lessons
- biased examples or assessments
- privacy exposure through learner data or practice artifacts
- over-personalized tutoring without enough oversight
- false confidence from automated grading
- accessibility failures hidden behind polished output
- credentialing people from weak or gameable assessments
- content drift away from authoritative sources

Learning and teaching management should define allowed AI uses, source-grounding requirements, human review, learner-data boundaries, assessment limits, disclosure posture, and monitoring for drift or harm.

AI can help draft a lesson. It cannot certify that someone is ready to operate a system, teach a class, handle a customer, comply with a regulation, or make a high-stakes decision just because it emitted a rubric with a reassuring tone.

## Collaboration Interfaces

Learning and teaching management often interfaces with:

- people management
- team management
- change management
- knowledge management
- operations management
- quality management
- compliance and regulatory management
- security management
- risk management
- product management
- sales management
- support and service management
- customer management
- data and analytics management
- culture management
- vendor and partner management
- research management

Clarify who owns the learning system, who owns the underlying work standard, who owns learner support, who owns performance decisions, and who can approve readiness. Otherwise training becomes the place every other discipline sends its unresolved problems to retire.

## Learning and Teaching Practitioner Boundary

For detailed practitioner scope, see [Learning / Teaching Practitioner](https://github.com/RusDavies/docs-practitioner-practices/blob/master/roles/learning-teaching-practitioner.md).


Learning and teaching practitioners perform hands-on work:

- teaching and facilitation
- instructional design
- curriculum development
- coaching and mentoring support
- learner assessment execution
- cohort and workshop operation
- learning content drafting and upkeep
- learner support and feedback collection
- practice-environment setup
- learning artifact production

Learning and teaching management owns the management frame:

- what capability matters and why
- how learning is prioritized and resourced
- what standards, accessibility requirements, and approvals apply
- what assessment or readiness gates are required
- how learning data is used and protected
- how learning connects to managers, operations, knowledge, change, compliance, quality, customer outcomes, or strategy

A person may manage and teach in a small organization. The role boundary still matters. Designing the course, teaching it, grading it, certifying readiness, and declaring the program effective should not all be hidden inside one unchecked act of self-congratulation.

## Anti-Patterns

Watch for these failure modes:

- treating every performance problem as a training problem
- measuring completion instead of capability
- building courses without defining the work people must perform
- creating generic learning paths that ignore role, risk, or context
- making training mandatory without making it useful
- confusing content delivery with teaching
- skipping practice, feedback, and reinforcement
- assessing recall when the job requires judgment
- using certificates as decoration without readiness evidence
- letting learning content drift away from authoritative sources
- using inaccessible formats or exclusionary participation norms
- turning compliance training into annual checkbox theatre
- asking managers to develop people without giving them methods, materials, or time
- using AI-generated learning content without source review
- using AI tutoring or grading without learner-data and assessment controls
- blaming learners for failing inside a badly designed learning system

## Practical Starting Point

For a team, function, school, community, or organization with weak learning and teaching management, start with:

1. Name the capabilities that matter most.
2. Identify the learner groups, current gaps, and risk if the gaps remain.
3. Define learning outcomes in observable behaviour, artifacts, or decisions.
4. Create a simple role-based learning path for one high-priority area.
5. Add practice, feedback, and assessment before adding more content.
6. Name owners for curriculum, teaching quality, content freshness, and readiness gates.
7. Connect learning content to authoritative knowledge sources.
8. Define AI-use, learner-data, accessibility, and assessment guardrails.
9. Review whether learning transfers into real work after the program runs.

Learning and teaching management is not about making everyone sit through more training. It is about building capability deliberately enough that people can do the work, improve the work, and teach the next person without relying on luck, folklore, or a heroic instructor with twelve tabs open.
